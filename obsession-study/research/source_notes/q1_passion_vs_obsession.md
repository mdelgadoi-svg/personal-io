# Q1 notes: how the literature defines and measures passion vs obsession, and the language markers each framework implies

Prepared Nov 17, 2026. Every citation detail below was retrieved in this session from Crossref, OpenAlex, Semantic Scholar, Europe PMC or PubMed Central. Quotation marks inside quotes were normalized to straight quotes; nothing else was changed. Raw files are in (session scratch folder).

Access in short: 17 sources at FULL TEXT, 12 at ABSTRACT ONLY, 3 at METADATA ONLY. ## 1. Answer

**Passion and "obsession" are not opposites in this literature. Obsessive passion is a kind of passion.** The dualistic model defines passion as "a strong inclination toward an activity that people like, that they find important, and in which they invest time and energy" [Vallerand2003]. The two types differ in how the activity became part of the person's identity. Harmonious passion (HP) comes from autonomous internalization and "leads individuals to choose to engage in the activity"; obsessive passion (OP) comes from controlled internalization that "creates an internal pressure to engage in the activity that the person likes" [Vallerand2003]. Both types involve love of the activity. The axis that separates them is control and fit with the rest of life: OP "leads people to experience an uncontrollable urge" [Vallerand2021], brings "negative affect and rigid persistence" [Vallerand2003], conflict with other parts of life [TothKiraly2017; Berg2020], and self-esteem that rises and falls with performance [Mageau2011].

**Measurement.** The Passion Scale has two subscales. The original has 14 items, 7 per type [Zhu2025; StCyr2021]; the revised version validated by Marsh et al. has "two 6-item subscales" [Vallerand2021; Marsh2013]. Marsh et al. tested it on N = 3,571 and found the same items work across leisure, sport, social, work and education, in French and English [Marsh2013]. HP items speak of harmony, integration, variety and discovery. OP items speak of an urge that is hard to control, exclusivity ("the only thing"), being controlled by the activity, emotional dependence and mood [TothKiraly2017; Berg2020; Zhu2025; Tu2022]. Two cautions matter for coding. First, HP and OP correlate positively (r = 0.587 to 0.718 in one large sample), so they must be coded as two separate dimensions [TothKiraly2017]. Second, the one item that uses the word "obsessive" also loads positively on harmonious passion [TothKiraly2017], and people low in OP overall still endorse it [Doistua2022]. The word "obsessed" alone is therefore a weak marker.

**Passion as expression (Jachimowicz group).** Jachimowicz et al. define passion as "a strong feeling toward a personally important value/preference that motivates intentions and behaviors to express that value/preference", an intense affective state "not necessarily limited to positive emotions alone" [Jachimowicz2018]. Expressing passion earns status and support from observers, with limits [Jachimowicz2019]. Krautter et al. show that the stereotypical expressions ("animated facial expressions, energetic body movements, varied tone, and pitch") are only a subset, more common in extraverts, and they built a broader Passion Expressions and Behaviors Scale (PEBS); extraverts express passion more often and in more ways and are seen as more passionate [Krautter2025]. I could open only the abstract, so the PEBS items, factors and the verbal versus nonverbal split are unverified (see section 3). What the abstract does establish: the stereotypical channel is entirely nonverbal or vocal, so a transcript cannot see it.

**Entrepreneurial passion.** Cardon et al. tie passion to "salient entrepreneurial role identities" [Cardon2009]; their definition, as reprinted, is "consciously accessible intense positive feelings experienced by engagement in entrepreneurial activities associated with roles that are meaningful and salient to the self-identity of the entrepreneur", across inventing, founding and developing [Li2022; Fu2022]. Their measure pairs intense positive feelings with identity centrality ("... is an important part of who I am") [Zhou2022]. Chen et al. separate displayed passion from preparedness and found "preparedness, not passion, positively impacted decisions to fund ventures" [Chen2009]; the passion items are all face, body and voice, while the preparedness items are about content [Yang2021].

**"Obsession" as a lay or investor word.** I found no peer-reviewed paper that defines "founder obsession" or "customer obsession" and separates it from clinical obsession and from obsessive passion. The closest is a conceptual paper proposing that entrepreneurs may have venture-related obsessions "similar to obsession as described in the clinical literature", which itself says the construct still needs defining [Fisher2013]. Clinical obsessions are "intrusive and unwanted" thoughts [SwierkoszLenart2023], which is a different thing from OP, where the activity is loved. Observers read OP as both morally suspect and a sign of "dedication, drive" [GamezDjokic2026], and two entrepreneur samples link OP to commitment and performance [Fisher2018; Tu2022].

### A. Vallerand's dualistic model and the Passion Scale

**[Vallerand2003]** Vallerand, R. J., Blanchard, C., Mageau, G. A., Koestner, R., Ratelle, C., Leonard, M., Gagne, M., & Marsolais, J. (2003). Les passions de l'ame: On obsessive and harmonious passion. Journal of Personality and Social Psychology, 85(4), 756-767. DOI 10.1037/0022-3514.85.4.756. PubMed 14561128.
- Access: ABSTRACT ONLY. Retrieved: `https://api.openalex.org/works/https://doi.org/10.1037/0022-3514.85.4.756` and Europe PMC search (DOI).
- Passion defined: "Passion is defined as a strong inclination toward an activity that people like, that they find important, and in which they invest time and energy."
- OP: "Obsessive passion (OP) refers to a controlled internalization of an activity in one's identity that creates an internal pressure to engage in the activity that the person likes."
- HP: "Harmonious passion (HP) refers to an autonomous internalization that leads individuals to choose to engage in the activity that they like."
- Outcomes and sample: "HP promotes healthy adaptation whereas OP thwarts it by causing negative affect and rigid persistence. Results from four studies involving more than 900 participants from different populations supported the proposed conceptualization."

**[Marsh2013]** Marsh, H. W., Vallerand, R. J., Lafreniere, M.-A. K., Parker, P., Morin, A. J. S., Carbonneau, N., Jowett, S., Bureau, J. S., Fernet, C., Guay, F., Salah Abduljabbar, A., & Paquet, Y. (2013). Passion: Does one scale fit all? Construct validity of two-factor passion scale and psychometric invariance over different activities and languages. Psychological Assessment, 25(3), 796-809. DOI 10.1037/a0032573. PubMed 23647035.
- Access: ABSTRACT ONLY. Retrieved: OpenAlex (DOI) and Europe PMC search (DOI).
- Sample and aim: "we evaluate the construct validity (factor structure, reliability, convergent and discriminant validity) of Passion Scale responses (N = 3,571)."
- Works across activities: "the same set of items is appropriate for assessing passion across a wide variety of activities".
- Language: "the instrument was originally devised in French, then translated into English".
- Validity correlates: "life satisfaction, rumination, conflict, time investment, activity liking and valuation, and perceiving the activity as a passion."

**[Vallerand2021]** Vallerand, R. J., Paquette, V., & Richard, C. (2021). The Role of Passion in Psychological and Cardiovascular Responses: Extending the Field of Passion and Positive Psychology in New Directions. Frontiers in Psychology, 12, 744629. DOI 10.3389/fpsyg.2021.744629. PMC8792226.
- Access: FULL TEXT. Retrieved: `https://www.ebi.ac.uk/europepmc/webservices/rest/PMC8792226/fullTextXML`. Used because Vallerand is first author and it states the model in his own words in an open paper.
- HP: "Harmonious passion (HP) originates from an autonomous internalization of the activity in identity and leads people to choose to engage in the activity that they love."
- OP: "obsessive passion (OP) originates from a controlled internalization in identity and leads people to experience an uncontrollable urge to engage in the activity that they love."
- The dark side: "passion can also arouse negative emotions, lead to inflexible persistence, and interfere with achieving a balanced, successful life."
- Scale: "The Passion Scale (Vallerand et al., 2003; Marsh et al., 2013) consists of two 6-item subscales assessing OP (e.g., 'I have difficulties controlling my urge to do my studies' ...), and HP (e.g., 'My studies are well integrated in my life' ...)."

**[TothKiraly2017]** Toth-Kiraly, I., Bothe, B., Rigo, A., & Orosz, G. (2017). An Illustration of the Exploratory Structural Equation Modeling (ESEM) Framework on the Passion Scale. Frontiers in Psychology, 8, 1968. DOI 10.3389/fpsyg.2017.01968. PMC5681952.
- Access: FULL TEXT. Retrieved: Europe PMC fullTextXML for PMC5681952. Samples: N = 7,466 and N = 504 Hungarian adults (abstract).
- OP mechanism: "the individual loses control over the activity and feels an uncontrollable pressure to engage in it, often indirectly creating conflicts with other aspects of life."
- Contingencies: "inter- or intra-personal contingencies are attached to the activity engagement, such as the maintenance of self-esteem or social acceptance".
- HP and OP are positively correlated: "factor correlations were also reduced for the ESEM (r = 0.587) model relative to the CFA (r = 0.718)."
- The "obsessive" item is ambiguous: OP4 and OP12 "differentially tap into HP (i.e., OP4 positively, whereas OP12 negatively)". OP4 is "I have almost an obsessive feeling for this activity".
- Item wording quoted in this paper (standard English wording): HP1 "This activity is in harmony with the other activities in my life"; HP10 "My activity is in harmony with other things that are part of me"; OP4 "I have almost an obsessive feeling for this activity"; OP7 "This activity is the only thing that really turns me on"; OP9 "If I could, I would only do my activity"; OP12 "I have the impression that my activity controls me". On OP7 and OP9: "both refer to the exclusive place that the activity occupies in one's life."

**[Berg2020]** Berg, S., Forest, J., & Stenseng, F. (2020). When Passion Does Not Change, but Emotions Do: Testing a Social Media Intervention Related to Exercise Activity Engagement. Frontiers in Psychology, 11, 71. DOI 10.3389/fpsyg.2020.00071. PMC7014931.
- Access: FULL TEXT. Retrieved: Europe PMC fullTextXML for PMC7014931.

- OP: "the person experiences an obsessive need to do the activity, often at the expense of other important aspects in life."

**[Zhu2025]** Zhu, M., He, K., & Qiang, Z. (2025). Why does job insecurity influence employees proactivity differently? A dual-path model based on dualistic passion. Frontiers in Psychology, 16, 1697231. DOI 10.3389/fpsyg.2025.1697231. PMC12740867.

**[Mageau2011]** Mageau, G. A., Carpentier, J., & Vallerand, R. J. (2011). The role of self-esteem contingencies in the distinction between obsessive and harmonious passion. European Journal of Social Psychology, 41(6), 720-729. DOI 10.1002/ejsp.798.
- Access: ABSTRACT ONLY. Retrieved: OpenAlex (DOI).

**Language markers this implies (Vallerand framework)**

Harmonious passion, from the source wording (scale items and definitions above):
- Harmony and fit with the rest of life: "in harmony with the other activities in my life", "well integrated in my life", "in harmony with other things that are part of me".
- Free choice and control: "choose to engage"; "a passion, that I still manage to control"; "really want to" (vs "have to").
- Growth and variety: "the new things that I discover", "a variety of experiences", "memorable experiences".
- Self-expression: "reflects the qualities I like about myself".
- Absorption without loss of control: "I am completely taken with" (this is an HP item in the original scale, so intensity alone is not obsessive).

Harmonious passion, my inference (not in the source wording):
- Naming other valued life domains as protected (family, sleep, hobbies, friends) and stopping or pausing by choice ("I took a week off", "I can switch off").
- Flexible persistence: changing course or quitting a failing path without self-blame. (The sources say OP is "rigid" and HP "flexible" [Berg2020]; the specific phrases are mine.)
- Enjoyment words about the work itself (love, fun, curiosity). Note both types involve love, so these mark passion in general more than HP.

Obsessive passion, from the source wording:
- Urge and loss of control: "The urge is so strong. I can't help myself", "difficulties controlling my urge", "tough time controlling my need", "so exciting that I sometimes lose control over it", "my activity controls me", "cannot help but engage".
- Exclusivity: "the only thing that really turns me on", "If I could, I would only do my activity".
- Dependence: "I cannot live without", "difficulty imagining my life without", "emotionally dependent", "My mood depends on me being able to do".
- Pressure language: "internal pressure", "feel you have to", "feel you must".
- Conflict and cost: "at the expense of other important aspects in life", neglecting studies or a partner, regret after choosing the activity over friends.
- Rigid persistence: continuing "even when negative outcomes are inevitable" (for example when injured).
- Contingent self-worth: self-esteem that fluctuates with performance; needing "social recognition" or "social acceptance".
- Rumination and negative affect tied to the activity.
- The literal word "obsessive" ("almost an obsessive feeling"), but treat as weak: see the two cautions in the Answer.

Obsessive passion, my inference:
- Guilt or anxiety when not working, sacrifice of sleep or health, "I am my company" statements, "nothing else matters". These fit the model's logic (pressure, conflict, contingent self-worth, negative affect) but none of these exact ideas is a Passion Scale item. Guilt in particular is not in any text I opened.
- Long hours alone. Time investment is a criterion for passion in general in this model ("invest time and energy"), not for OP specifically.

### B. Krautter, Buechner and Jachimowicz: passion expression and the PEBS

**Language markers this implies (Krautter framework)**

From the source wording (abstract):
- None that a transcript can show. The only expressions named in the abstract are face, body and voice: "animated facial expressions, energetic body movements, varied tone, and pitch".

My inference:
- A transcript removes the stereotypical channel, so a transcript-based passion score measures only the content channel (what is said). That is a different slice of expression from what investors see in a room or on video.
- If the PEBS includes verbal or behavioral categories (for example saying how much the work matters, talking about it often, putting in extra effort), those would be transcript-visible. This is a guess about a scale I could not read. Do not cite PEBS factors or items until someone reads the paper.
- Extraversion is a likely confound for any expressed-passion score, including a language one (talkativeness, word count, emotional words).

### C. Jachimowicz and colleagues: passion as a strong feeling toward a personal value

**[Jachimowicz2018]** Jachimowicz, J. M., Wihler, A., Bailey, E. R., & Galinsky, A. D. (2018). Why grit requires perseverance and passion to positively predict performance. Proceedings of the National Academy of Sciences, 115(40), 9980-9985. DOI 10.1073/pnas.1803561115. PubMed 30224491. PMC6176608.
- Access: FULL TEXT. Retrieved: `https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6176608/`.
- Definition: "we define passion as a strong feeling toward a personally important value/preference that motivates intentions and behaviors to express that value/preference."
- Not only positive: "passion is an intense affective state, but one that is not necessarily limited to positive emotions alone".
- Passion attainment: "whether people experience desired levels of passion". Items (reverse-scored): "I am less passionate for my work than I should be," "I often feel as if I have to be more passionate for my work," and "I frequently feel obliged to be more passionate for my work than I currently am."
- Evidence: meta-analysis of "127 studies (n = 45,485)"; employees (n = 422) and students (n = 248); "perseverance without passion attainment is mere drudgery". The definition draws on Chen 2009, Cardon 2009, Vallerand 2003 and Perrewe et al. 2014 (their refs 21 to 24). They also measured harmonious passion with Vallerand's subscale as a control ("My work is in harmony with the other activities in my life").
- Limits they state: "The study designs were cross-sectional".

**[Jachimowicz2019]** Jachimowicz, J. M., To, C., Agasi, S., Cote, S., & Galinsky, A. D. (2019). The gravitational pull of expressing passion: When and how expressing passion elicits status conferral and support from others. Organizational Behavior and Human Decision Processes, 153, 41-62. DOI 10.1016/j.obhdp.2019.06.002. Preprint DOI 10.31234/osf.io/uktne.
- Access: ABSTRACT ONLY. Retrieved: Semantic Scholar (DOI) for the abstract; Crossref and OpenAlex for the citation.
- Core claim: "observers confer status on and support those who express passion."
- Evidence base: "Six studies, including entrepreneurial pitches from Dragons' Den and two pre-registered experiments".
- Admired, not liked (my paraphrase, because the original sentence is punctuated with dashes): expressing passion affected how admired someone was, but not how accepted.
- Limits: effects "were weaker when passion was expressed in an inappropriate manner/context, and when observers disagreed with the target of expresser's passion"; "in competitive contexts, expressing passion became threatening".

**Language markers this implies (Jachimowicz framework)**

From the source wording:
- Statements that something is personally important or valued ("a personally important value/preference"), and a wish to act on it ("consistently desire engaging and interacting with" the target).
- Strong feeling that need not be positive (so anger or frustration on behalf of the cause can count as passion).
- Passion shortfall: "less passionate ... than I should be", "have to be more passionate", "obliged to be more passionate".

My inference:
- "I care deeply about", "this matters to me because", "I believe in", values and mission talk tied to the self; and for shortfall, "I lost the spark", "I had to force myself".
- Obligation wording about passion itself ("should", "have to", "obliged") sits close to the pressure language of obsessive passion. A codebook should keep "obliged to feel passion" (attainment gap) apart from "driven by an urge I cannot control" (OP).

### D. Entrepreneurial passion: Cardon, and Chen, Yao and Kotha

**[Cardon2009]** Cardon, M. S., Wincent, J., Singh, J., & Drnovsek, M. (2009). The nature and experience of entrepreneurial passion. Academy of Management Review, 34(3), 511-532. DOI 10.5465/amr.2009.40633190.
- Access: ABSTRACT ONLY. Retrieved: OpenAlex (DOI); Crossref for the citation.
- "We build on fragmented and disparate extant work to conceptualize the nature of entrepreneurial passion associated with salient entrepreneurial role identities."
- "We also theorize the mechanisms of the experience of entrepreneurial passion that provide coherence to goal-directed cognitions and behaviors during the pursuit of entrepreneurial effectiveness."
- The formal definition is not in the abstract. It is reprinted in [Li2022] and [Fu2022] below.

**[Cardon2013]** Cardon, M. S., Gregoire, D. A., Stevens, C. E., & Patel, P. C. (2013). Measuring entrepreneurial passion: Conceptual foundations and scale validation. Journal of Business Venturing, 28(3), 373-396. DOI 10.1016/j.jbusvent.2012.03.003.
- Access: METADATA ONLY (Crossref, OpenAlex and Semantic Scholar return no abstract). Exists, not read. Everything about its measure below comes from secondary reprints.

**[Li2022]** Li, M., Makino, S., Luo, L., & Jiang, C. (2022). Entrepreneurial passion and organizational innovation: The moderating effects of events and the competence to exploit events. Asia Pacific Journal of Management. DOI 10.1007/s10490-022-09853-6. PMC9713172.
- Access: FULL TEXT. Retrieved: Europe PMC fullTextXML for PMC9713172.
- Reprints the definition with a page number: "Following Cardon et al. (2009: 517), we define entrepreneurial passion as 'consciously accessible intense positive feelings experienced by engagement in entrepreneurial activities associated with roles that are meaningful and salient to the self-identity of the entrepreneur.'"

**Language markers this implies (Cardon and Chen frameworks)**

From the source wording:
- Cardon, intense positive feelings about a specific founder role: "exciting", "enjoyable", "excites me", "energizes me", "motivated to figure out", tied to inventing (new ideas, new solutions, unmet market needs), founding (establishing or owning the company) or developing (nurturing and growing companies).
- Cardon, identity centrality: "... is an important part of who I am", in the same three roles.
- Chen, passion: none visible in a transcript. All four reprinted items are face, body and voice.
- Chen, preparedness (visible in a transcript): substance, depth, coherence and logic, linking the plan to the broader context, citing facts.

My inference:
- "I am a builder", "I have always been a founder", "this is who I am" as identity-centrality markers; role-specific coding (which of the three roles the positive feeling is about).
- Identity centrality is shared ground between Cardon's passion and Vallerand's OP (identity plus contingent self-worth). "The company is part of who I am" marks passion in Cardon's sense. It tips toward OP only when worth depends on it ("if this fails, I am nothing"). That boundary is my reading, not a tested rule.
- Preparedness is a separate code from passion and should be coded separately, since Chen found it, not displayed passion, predicted funding.

## 4. Implications for the study design

- **Code two dimensions, not one scale from "passionate" to "obsessed".** HP and OP correlate positively (r = 0.587 to 0.718 in [TothKiraly2017]) and both rest on loving the activity. Each transcript segment should get a separate HP score and OP score, plus a general passion or intensity score.
- **Do not let the word "obsessed" drive the obsessive code.** The one Passion Scale item with the word "obsessive" leans toward harmonious passion too, and people low in OP endorse it [TothKiraly2017; Doistua2022]. Make "lay obsession word" its own code and require control, exclusivity, dependence, conflict or contingent self-worth language for OP.
- **Anchor the codebook in item wording that can be cited.** The 14 original items [Zhu2025; Tu2022] and the 12 revised items give ready-made, citable anchors: control vs "can't help myself", harmony vs "the only thing", "manage to control" vs "controls me". Markers such as guilt, sleep loss and health sacrifice should be labelled as the study's own extension, and the human coders' agreement on them reported separately.
- **Tell the advisor plainly what a transcript cannot see.** The best-known passion displays are face, body and voice [Krautter2025; Chen2009 via Yang2021]. A transcript-based measure captures content only. This is a limit, and also a possible strength: it may be less tied to extraversion. Add a control for talkativeness (words spoken) and, if possible, a text-based extraversion proxy.
- **Read Krautter et al. in full before fixing the codebook.** The PEBS is the closest existing tool for the ways passion shows up beyond the stereotype, and its verbal categories (if any) should be mapped onto the codes. This is the one paper worth a manual download.
- **Add preparedness as a rival code.** Chen et al. found that preparedness, not displayed passion, predicted funding [Chen2009]. Coding substance, logic and use of facts from the same transcripts lets the study test passion and obsession language against the obvious alternative.
- **Code the role and the identity statement.** Cardon's framework says which role the feeling attaches to (inventing, founding, developing) and whether it is "an important part of who I am" [Zhou2022; Li2022]. Identity centrality overlaps with OP's contingent self-worth [Mageau2011], so the codebook needs a clear rule for where "this is who I am" ends and "I am nothing without it" begins.
- **State the validity gap honestly.** The Passion Scale is self-report about one's own inner experience; the study codes public speech to an audience. Founders may perform "obsession" because investors reward it [GamezDjokic2026; Jachimowicz2019]. The measure is best described as "obsessive-passion language" or expressed passion, not as the founder's actual passion type, unless a subsample also fills in the Passion Scale.
