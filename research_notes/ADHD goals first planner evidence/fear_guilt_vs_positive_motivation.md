# Fear, guilt, anxiety and trauma as motivators (especially in ADHD) vs goal-based, self-chosen motivation, and what each costs

Notes for Marcelo (graduate student at UC Berkeley with ADHD, building a planner for people with ADHD). His beliefs are tested in the last section. Evidence strength labels used throughout: **Strong** (several meta-analyses or large preregistered trials that agree), **Moderate** (one meta-analysis, or several consistent but correlational studies), **Weak** (small, cross-sectional, qualitative, child-only, or indirect).

Method note: most claims come from abstracts, publisher pages, PubMed records and press releases found by web search. Full texts on pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov could not be opened in this session (the host is outside the session's allowed network); numbers I could not confirm are flagged.

## 1. Survivorship and selection bias: what do trauma, ACEs and PTSD do to people with ADHD on average, and what do studies of "successful" adults with ADHD show?

### Takeaway
On average, childhood adversity and trauma go with more ADHD, more PTSD and more impairment, not with success; the successful, fear-driven people Marcelo sees at Berkeley are a filtered sample (those who were derailed are not there). Studies of successful adults with ADHD are tiny and qualitative, and the strengths they report are energy, curiosity, courage and resilience, not fear. Evidence strength: Moderate for the average harms (large meta-analyses, but observational), Weak for anything about "successful" subgroups.

### Cited Findings
- **ACEs and ADHD (meta-analysis):** Zhang, N. & Gao, M. (2022). Understanding the association between adverse childhood experiences and subsequent ADHD: a systematic review and meta-analysis of observational studies. *Brain and Behavior*. Pooled OR = 1.68 (95% CI 1.54 to 1.83); about 70 studies and nearly 4 million participants; stronger for people with multiple ACEs and for females. The association may run both ways (ADHD also raises the risk of experiencing adversity). [PMC9575611](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC9575611/); [PubMed 36068993](https://www.pubmed.gov/36068993/)
- **PTSD and ADHD (meta-analysis):** Spencer, A. E., Faraone, S. V., Bogucki, O. E., et al. (2016). Examining the association between posttraumatic stress disorder and attention-deficit/hyperactivity disorder: a systematic review and meta-analysis. *Journal of Clinical Psychiatry*, 77(1), 72-83. Relative risk of PTSD in ADHD = 2.9 (3.7 vs healthy controls; 1.6 vs traumatized controls). Relative risk of ADHD in PTSD = 1.7 (2.1 vs traumatized controls). The authors read this as bidirectional, with ADHD raising vulnerability to PTSD after trauma, not only more trauma exposure. [J Clin Psychiatry PDF](https://www.psychiatrist.com/wp-content/uploads/2021/02/13429_examining-association-between-posttraumatic-stress.pdf); [PubMed 26114394](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/26114394/). An independent evidence summary rated the ADHD-in-PTSD evidence "moderate quality" showing "a small increase" in ADHD rates. [NeuRA technical commentary](https://library.neura.edu.au/wp-content/uploads/2021/08/Technical_commentary_ADHD.pdf)
- **Adult ADHD and life outcomes (population survey):** Kessler, R. C., Adler, L., Barkley, R., et al. (2006). The prevalence and correlates of adult ADHD in the United States: results from the National Comorbidity Survey Replication. *American Journal of Psychiatry*, 163(4), 716-723. Screen in a probability subsample aged 18 to 44 (n = 3,199); prevalence 4.4%; correlates include being unemployed and previously married; substantial role impairment; most cases untreated. [PubMed 16585449](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/16585449/); [AJP](https://ajp.psychiatryonline.org/doi/10.1176/ajp.2006.163.4.716)
- **College students with ADHD:** a survey of 2,197 students at four universities notes that undergraduates with ADHD experience more academic impairment and are less likely to graduate. (I saw only the abstract; the link-to-study match comes from search results.) [PubMed 36924424](https://www.pubmed.gov/36924424/)
- **"Some adversity" is not "trauma":** Seery, M. D., Holman, E. A. & Silver, R. C. (2010). Whatever does not kill us: cumulative lifetime adversity, vulnerability, and resilience. *Journal of Personality and Social Psychology*, 99(6), 1025-1041. US national panel, n = 2,398, assessed 2001 to 2004. U-shaped results: people with *some* lifetime adversity had lower distress, less functional impairment, fewer PTSD symptoms and higher life satisfaction than people with *none* or *high* adversity, and were least affected by new adverse events. Linear harms of more adversity were also present; correlational. [PubMed 20939649](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/20939649/); [University at Buffalo release](https://www.buffalo.edu/news/releases/2010/10/11868.html)
- **Successful adults with ADHD (qualitative):** Sedgwick, J. A., Merwood, A. & Asherson, P. (2019). The positive aspects of attention deficit hyperactivity disorder: a qualitative investigation of successful adults with ADHD. *ADHD Attention Deficit and Hyperactivity Disorders*, 11, 241-253. **n = 6** employed men from a London NHS tertiary clinic, interpretive phenomenological interviews. Six themes: cognitive dynamism, courage, energy, humanity, resilience, transcendence. The authors flag the small, all-male sample. [Springer](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s12402-018-0277-6)
- **Compensation strategies (qualitative):** Canela, C., Buadze, A., Dube, A., Eich, D. & Liebrenz, M. (2017). Skills and compensation strategies in adult ADHD: a qualitative study. *PLoS ONE*, 12(9), e0184964. n = 32 outpatients, university hospital specialty unit, Zurich; semi-structured interviews. Many strategies of varying effectiveness, grouped as organizational, motoric, attentional, social and psychopharmacological (a majority saw nicotine as a way to reduce symptoms); in some circumstances ADHD symptoms were seen as useful. Coping before diagnosis may help explain late diagnosis. [PMC5617155](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC5617155/)
- **Self-compassion and criticism in adults with ADHD:** Beaton, D. M., Sirois, F. & Milne, E. (2020). Self-compassion and perceived criticism in adults with ADHD. *Mindfulness*, 11, 2506-2518. n = 1,203 adults (46% self-reported ADHD diagnosis), online, cross-sectional. Adults with ADHD had lower self-compassion and higher perceived criticism than adults without; high-trait undiagnosed adults looked like the diagnosed group; perceived criticism partly explained the low self-compassion. A follow-up in *Journal of Clinical Psychology* linked low self-compassion to poorer mental health in ADHD. A randomized trial of a self-compassion intervention is registered (NCT04978792); I found no results. [White Rose eprint](https://eprints.whiterose.ac.uk/165039/); [ClinicalTrials.gov](https://clinicaltrials.gov/study/NCT04978792)

### Inferences
- His observation (successful ADHD peers with deep trauma who follow rules out of fear) is a textbook selection effect: Berkeley admits only those who succeeded, so the group cannot show how many traumatized people with ADHD were derailed. The population data point the other way (ACEs OR about 1.7 for ADHD; PTSD risk about 3 times higher in ADHD; adult ADHD linked to unemployment and lower graduation).
- A charitable reading that the evidence does allow: fear-driven compliance (introjected regulation, section 3) does predict effort and persistence, and moderate (not severe) adversity is associated with resilience (Seery). So "fear produced effort in these people" is plausible; "trauma made them successful" is not supported.
- Criticism and low self-compassion are common in adults with ADHD (Beaton), so many of his peers may have learned self-criticism as a motor; that is a cost to measure, not a model to copy.

### Gaps
- I found no study of "high-achieving" or elite-university adults with ADHD that measured fear, anxiety or trauma as drivers of success.
- I did not confirm whether Canela et al. (2017) list "using pressure, deadlines or panic" as a strategy; reading the full text (PMC5617155) would settle it.
- No pooled estimate found for ACEs and educational attainment in ADHD samples. The general-population ACE meta-analysis (Hughes et al., 2017, *Lancet Public Health*) was not retrieved.

## 2. Anxiety comorbidity in ADHD: how common is it, and does anxiety reduce impulsivity and help functioning, or make it worse? (Yerkes-Dodson style arguments)

### Takeaway
Anxiety disorders affect roughly half of adults with ADHD. A few lab studies find anxiety slightly improves response inhibition, and in the child MTA trial anxious children responded well to behavioral treatment, but in adults comorbid anxiety mostly adds impairment. The Yerkes-Dodson "inverted U" is weaker science than its popularity suggests. Evidence strength: Moderate for prevalence; Weak and mixed for "anxiety helps inhibition"; Moderate that anxiety adds impairment.

### Cited Findings
- **Prevalence in adults:** Kessler et al. (2006) NCS-R analysis (above). A secondary paper reports 47.1% of adults with ADHD also met criteria for an anxiety disorder, most often social phobia, specific phobia and PTSD, and calls it lifetime. [PMC6493806](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6493806); a roundtable summary gives about 47% as comorbid anxiety in the past 12 months. [PMC1896299](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC1896299). The two secondary sources conflict on the time frame; the original table should be checked. Kessler et al. say ADHD's average comorbidity odds ratios are comparable to those of most anxiety and mood disorders. [AJP](https://ajp.psychiatryonline.org/doi/10.1176/ajp.2006.163.4.716)
- **MTA (CHILD ONLY, ages 7 to 9.9):** March, J. S., Swanson, J. M., Arnold, L. E., et al. (2000). Anxiety as a predictor and outcome variable in the Multimodal Treatment Study of Children with ADHD (MTA). *Journal of Abnormal Child Psychology*, 28(6), 527-541. 33% met DSM-III-R criteria for an anxiety disorder (excluding simple phobia). Parent-reported anxiety moderated outcome in favor of psychosocial (behavioral) treatment, mostly on parent-reported disruptive behavior, internalizing symptoms and inattention; no adverse effect of anxiety on medication response. [FIU record](https://discovery.fiu.edu/display/pub68575); [Duke record](https://scholars.duke.edu/publication/738526)
- **MTA comorbid subgroups (CHILD ONLY):** Jensen, P. S., et al. (2001), *JAACAP*, comparing comorbid subgroups, reported (per search summaries of the MTA literature) that children with ADHD plus anxiety but without ODD/CD responded about equally well to behavioral and medication treatment. Not verified from the original. [Duke record of March et al.](https://scholars.duke.edu/publication/738526)
- **Inhibition in adults is mixed:** a 2025 study of emerging adults found trait anxiety moderated the effect of ADHD (total and hyperactive-impulsive scores) only in the response inhibition condition. [Frontiers in Psychiatry 2025](https://www.frontiersin.org/articles/10.3389/fpsyt.2025.1597559/full). Literature reviews summarized in the same search describe both directions: in some studies anxiety counteracted ADHD executive problems (for example fewer commission errors on Go/NoGo in anxious adults), in others it worsened inhibitory control in children and adults. (Exact review not confirmed; treat as a pointer.)
- **Functional impairment in adults:** in 209 Brazilian young adults (ages 20 to 31), those with ADHD had more anxiety, depression and functional impairment, and anxiety appeared to mediate between ADHD severity and impairment (cross-sectional). [PMC9480188](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC9480188/)
- **Yerkes-Dodson critique:** Corbett, M. (2015). From law to folklore: work stress and the Yerkes-Dodson Law. *Journal of Managerial Psychology*, 30(6), 741-752. Traces how a 1908 animal experiment (mice learning under shocks) became a "law" of human performance and finds the empirical support "wanting." [Emerald](https://emerald.com/insight/content/doi/10.1108/JMP-03-2013-0085/full/html)

### Inferences
- "Anxiety keeps me in line" has a grain of lab support (anxiety can slightly raise caution on inhibition tasks), but in daily life comorbid anxiety tracks with more impairment, not less. A small inhibition gain does not offset the cost.
- The MTA moderation is about which *treatment* works best for anxious children (behavioral support), not evidence that anxiety itself helps. It is child-only.
- Urgency near a deadline (the "Delay" term in temporal motivation theory, section 7) is a better explanation for "panic works" than a Yerkes-Dodson curve: the deadline raises the value of acting now, and the anxiety is a side effect.

### Gaps
- The exact Kessler (2006) anxiety odds ratio and time frame were not confirmed (PMC full text blocked).
- I did not retrieve Arnsten (2009, *Nature Reviews Neuroscience*) on stress impairing prefrontal function, or attentional control theory (Eysenck et al., 2007), which predict that anxiety costs efficiency even when performance holds.
- No adult longitudinal study found that tests whether comorbid anxiety predicts better academic or career outcomes in ADHD.

## 3. Self-determination theory: autonomous vs controlled motivation, especially introjected regulation (guilt, shame, contingent self-worth)

### Takeaway
Large meta-analyses agree: guilt and ego-driven motivation (introjection) does buy persistence, but with more anxiety and ill-being; external pressure has small behavioral benefits and well-being costs; motivation from goals one personally values (identified regulation) is the best predictor of persistence and performance, and intrinsic enjoyment the best predictor of well-being. This is the strongest support for Marcelo's "negative vs positive incentive" idea, with one twist: the winner is "I value this goal," not "this is fun." SDT work on ADHD samples is thin. Evidence strength: Strong for general populations (hundreds of samples), Weak for ADHD specifically.

### Cited Findings
- **Students (meta-analysis):** Howard, J. L., Bureau, J. S., Guay, F., Chong, J. X. Y. & Ryan, R. M. (2021). Student motivation and associated outcomes: a meta-analysis from self-determination theory. *Perspectives on Psychological Science*, 16(6), 1300-1323. 344 samples, N = 223,209, 26 outcomes. Intrinsic motivation tracked success and well-being; identified regulation was especially tied to persistence; introjected (ego-involved) regulation was positively related to persistence and performance goals but also positively related to ill-being. [ACU record](https://acuresearchbank.acu.edu.au/item/8w8z7/student-motivation-and-associated-outcomes-a-meta-analysis-from-self-determination-theory); DOI 10.1177/1745691620966789
- **What produces autonomous motivation (meta-analysis):** Bureau, J. S., Howard, J. L., Chong, J. X. Y. & Guay, F. (2022). Pathways to student motivation: a meta-analysis of antecedents of autonomous and controlled motivations. *Review of Educational Research*. 144 studies, more than 79,000 students; asks which need (autonomy, competence, relatedness) best predicts self-determined motivation. [PDF](https://selfdeterminationtheory.org/wp-content/uploads/2021/09/2021_PathwaystoStudentMotiv_BureaHowardEtal.pdf)
- **Work (meta-analysis):** Van den Broeck, A., Howard, J. L., Van Vaerenbergh, Y., Leroy, H. & Gagne, M. (2021). Beyond intrinsic and extrinsic motivation: a meta-analysis on self-determination theory's multidimensional conceptualization of work motivation. *Organizational Psychology Review*, 11(3), 240-273. 124 samples. Intrinsic motivation most important for well-being and attitudes; identified regulation more powerful for performance and citizenship behavior; introjection has both positive and negative consequences; external regulation has limited links to behavior and has well-being costs; amotivation is only negative. [Monash record](https://research.monash.edu/en/publications/beyond-intrinsic-and-extrinsic-motivation-a-meta-analysis-on-self/)
- **Fear-of-mistakes perfectionism (meta-analysis):** Hill, A. P. & Curran, T. (2016). Multidimensional perfectionism and burnout: a meta-analysis. *Personality and Social Psychology Review*, 20(3), 269-288. 43 studies, N = 9,838, 663 effect sizes. Perfectionistic *concerns* (fear of mistakes, worry about others' judgment) had medium-to-large positive links with burnout; perfectionistic *strivings* had small negative or null links. Strongest in the work domain. [PubMed 26231736](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/26231736/)
- **SDT and ADHD (review):** "Studying motivation in ADHD: the role of internal motives and the relevance of self determination theory" argues SDT could enrich ADHD motivation research, which has mostly studied reward sensitivity. [PMC9066661](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC9066661/)
- **SDT and ADHD in college:** survey of N = 2,197 students at four universities on ADHD and psychological need satisfaction and frustration (abstract only seen). [PubMed 36924424](https://www.pubmed.gov/36924424/)
- **Autonomy is a double-edged sword in ADHD (school, not college):** a 2025 Norwegian interview study (two students with inattention and their teachers) found autonomy support helped students attend to their own needs (interest, breaks), but autonomy in tasks that rely on self-regulation "might constitute a double-edged sword." Very small, qualitative. [Frontiers in Psychology 2025](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/psychology/articles/10.3389/fpsyg.2025.1624279/full)

### Inferences
- "Fear works" and "fear is unhealthy" are both supported by SDT meta-analyses: introjection predicts persistence and ill-being at the same time. That is close to Marcelo's own words.
- His "positive incentive" (goals he chose that grow his capabilities) maps best to **identified regulation**, which predicts persistence and performance better than enjoyment does. The app's goals with a "why" are aimed at exactly this.
- SDT separates autonomy (endorsing what you do) from the absence of structure. For ADHD, the Norwegian finding and the deadline evidence (section 7) suggest structure should stay, but chosen and explained, not imposed.
- Fear-of-mistakes perfectionism is a well-measured form of fear-driven striving, and its cost (burnout) is medium-to-large.

### Gaps
- Exact effect sizes for introjected regulation by outcome (Howard et al. 2021; Van den Broeck et al. 2021) not retrieved.
- No SDT intervention trial with adults with ADHD found. Smith & Langberg reviews of college ADHD were not retrieved.
- Not retrieved: Teixeira et al. (2012) exercise-adherence review and other evidence that introjected motivation fades over time faster than identified motivation; this would directly test his "I want to quit" pattern.

## 4. Approach vs avoidance goals (Elliot & Sheldon 1997, Elliot & Church 1997, later meta-analyses)

### Takeaway
Goals framed as avoiding failure ("don't fail," "don't look bad") predict worse well-being and, modestly, worse performance than approach goals ("master this," "get this done"). Evidence strength: Moderate (consistent longitudinal and meta-analytic findings, small effects on performance).

### Cited Findings
- Elliot, A. J. & Sheldon, K. M. (1997). Avoidance achievement motivation: a personal goals analysis. *Journal of Personality and Social Psychology*, 73, 171-185. Over a semester, avoidance personal achievement goals had "deleterious consequences for a host of achievement-relevant and general well-being outcomes," including longitudinal decline in subjective well-being. [World Database of Happiness record](https://worlddatabaseofhappiness.eur.nl/publications/avoidance-achievement-motivation-a-personal-goals-analysis-10188/)
- Elliot, A. J., Sheldon, K. M. & Church, M. A. (1997). Avoidance personal goals and subjective well-being. *Personality and Social Psychology Bulletin*, 23, 915-927. Approach relative to avoidance goals contribute more strongly to well-being. [Record](https://worlddatabaseofhappiness.eur.nl/publications/avoidance-personal-goals-and-subjective-well-being-4370)
- Hulleman, C. S., Schrager, S. M., Bodmann, S. M. & Harackiewicz, J. M. (2010), *Psychological Bulletin* meta-analysis, as summarized in a 2021 meta-analysis: appearance-focused performance-approach goals were negatively related to performance while normative ones were positively related; later reviews describe both mastery-avoidance and performance-avoidance goals as generally negative for motivation and performance. [Social Psychology of Education 2021](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s11218-021-09606-1)
- A meta-analysis cited in the same search results (attributed to Huang) reports performance-avoidance correlations with achievement of about -.08 to -.20 depending on the scale; source not confirmed. [Educational Psychology Review](https://link.springer.com/doi/10.1007/s10648-011-9155-x)

### Inferences
- Rules followed "out of fear" are avoidance goals by definition. The cost shows up first in well-being, then modestly in performance.
- Turning a fear-framed task ("don't fail the exam") into an approach step ("read section 2 for 10 minutes") is a small, evidence-consistent rewrite the app already uses with first steps.

### Gaps
- Elliot & Church (1997, *JPSP* 72, 218-232) was not retrieved; its findings (performance-avoidance goals predicting lower grades and less intrinsic motivation) are from memory and are not cited here.
- Exact Hulleman et al. (2010) coefficients not confirmed.
- No approach/avoidance goal study in ADHD samples found.

## 5. Shame vs guilt (Tangney and colleagues): when does guilt about a behavior motivate repair, and when does shame about the self lead to avoidance?

### Takeaway
The classic view (Tangney) is that guilt about a specific act motivates repair while shame about the whole self drives hiding and escape. A large meta-analysis refines this: what matters most is whether the failure feels **reparable**. When it can be fixed, both shame and guilt push toward constructive action; when it cannot, shame turns to avoidance. Evidence strength: Moderate to Strong (90-sample meta-analysis), mostly adult.

### Cited Findings
- Tangney, J. P., Stuewig, J. & Mashek, D. J. (2007). Moral emotions and moral behavior. *Annual Review of Psychology*, 58, 345-372. Reviews the shame-guilt distinction and their relative advantages and costs, and the difference between anticipatory and consequential moral emotions. [PubMed 16953797](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/16953797/)
- Leach, C. W. & Cidam, A. (2015). When is shame linked to constructive approach orientation? A meta-analysis. *Journal of Personality and Social Psychology*, 109(6), 983-1002. Tested "the prevailing view of shame as avoidant." 90 samples, N = 12,364. Shame was positively linked to constructive approach when the failure was more reparable (g = .47, 95% CI .37 to .55) or the social image was reparable (g = .37), and negatively when the failure was less reparable (g = -.34, 95% CI -.53 to -.14). In a supplemental 42 samples, shame and guilt had similar positive links when failure was reparable (g = .44 and .43); when less reparable, shame g = -.08 and guilt g = .27. [PubMed 26641074](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/26641074/)
- Self-forgiveness and self-compassion research (section 7) shows that easing the bad feeling after procrastinating predicts less procrastination next time, which fits the reparability idea.

### Inferences
- Guilt is not always bad: guilt about a specific missed task, paired with an immediate, doable repair, is linked to constructive action. The harm comes when the failure feels permanent or global ("I am the kind of person who fails"), which is where many adults with ADHD live after years of criticism (Beaton et al., section 1).
- Design rule this supports: every "not yet" moment should come with one small reparable step (the app's voice rules already do this).

### Gaps
- No shame/guilt study in ADHD samples found in this session.
- Leach & Cidam's samples are mixed in culture and age; I did not check the share of college samples.

## 6. Fear appeals (Tannenbaum et al. 2015): the fair counterpoint

### Takeaway
Fear does change attitudes, intentions and behavior: on average a small-to-moderate effect (d = 0.29), with no conditions found where it backfired. It works best when paired with a clear, doable action (efficacy) and for one-time behaviors, less for repeated ones. Evidence strength: Strong for "fear persuades a little"; it says nothing about the long-term well-being cost of living on fear.

### Cited Findings
- Tannenbaum, M. B., Hepler, J., Zimmerman, R. S., Saul, L., Jacobs, S., Wilson, K. & Albarracin, D. (2015). Appealing to fear: a meta-analysis of fear appeal effectiveness and theories. *Psychological Bulletin*, 141(6), 1178-1204. 127 articles (9% unpublished), 248 independent samples, N = 27,372. Random-effects d = 0.29 on a composite of attitudes, intentions and behaviors. Stronger with more fear depicted, efficacy statements, high susceptibility and severity, recommendations for one-time (vs repeated) behaviors, and more women in the audience. "No identified circumstances under which they backfire." [PubMed 26501228](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/26501228/); [dissertation version](https://core.ac.uk/download/158301889.pdf); [APA press release](https://www.apa.org/news/press/releases/2015/10/fear-based-appeals)
- Albarracin, the senior author: "fear appeals should not be seen as a panacea because the effect is still small." [ScienceDaily](https://www.sciencedaily.com/releases/2015/10/151022094459.htm)

### Inferences
- This supports Marcelo's first claim that fear "works." The fair reading: fear plus a clear action moves behavior a bit, most reliably for single acts (get the vaccine, submit the form).
- Daily work is a repeated behavior, where fear appeals are weaker, and the studies measure short-run persuasion, not stress, burnout or quitting over months (sections 3 and 10 cover those costs).
- The efficacy moderator matches the shame meta-analysis: negative emotion helps only when a doable path is visible.

### Gaps
- No fear-appeal study in ADHD samples found. No study found comparing chronic self-directed fear (self-threats) with fear messages from others.

## 7. Procrastination: temporal motivation theory, mood repair, self-compassion, self-forgiveness, and whether guilt-free breaks help

### Takeaway
Procrastination is driven by task aversiveness, low confidence, impulsiveness and distance to the deadline, and it works as short-term mood repair. Guilt and self-criticism afterward feed the loop; self-forgiveness and self-compassion are linked to less procrastination later, and guilt about leisure ruins its recovery value. Short breaks restore energy but do not reliably raise performance. Evidence strength: Strong for the predictors (691-correlation meta-analysis); Moderate for self-compassion and self-forgiveness (correlational, small samples); Moderate for breaks; **no direct evidence** for "structured procrastination" or for games as a bridge back to work.

### Cited Findings
- **Meta-analysis and theory:** Steel, P. (2007). The nature of procrastination: a meta-analytic and theoretical review of quintessential self-regulatory failure. *Psychological Bulletin*, 133(1), 65-94. 691 correlations. Strong, consistent predictors: task aversiveness, task delay, self-efficacy, impulsiveness, and conscientiousness facets (self-control, distractibility, organization, achievement motivation). Neuroticism, rebelliousness and sensation seeking had only weak links. Steel integrates these in temporal motivation theory (Motivation = Expectancy x Value / (Impulsiveness x Delay)). [University of Calgary repository](https://prism.ucalgary.ca/handle/1880/47914). Steel in the press: procrastinators have "less expectancy that they can actually complete a task"; perfectionists "actually procrastinate less, but they worry about it more." [ScienceDaily 2007](https://www.sciencedaily.com/releases/2007/01/070110090851.htm)
- **ADHD and procrastination:** Niermann, H. C. M. & Scheres, A. (2014). The relation between procrastination and symptoms of ADHD in undergraduate students. *International Journal of Methods in Psychiatric Research*. n = 54 students with varying self-reported ADHD symptoms; after partial correlations only inattention (not hyperactivity-impulsivity) correlated with general procrastination; authors call it preliminary. [PDF](https://www.epanlab.nl/wp-content/uploads/2017/05/Niermann-Scheres-2014.pdf). A 2021 adult study found people with more ADHD symptoms reported more procrastination and internalizing symptoms. [Frontiers in Psychology 2021](https://www.frontiersin.org/articles/10.3389/fpsyg.2021.708579/pdf)
- **Mood repair:** Sirois, F. & Pychyl, T. (2013). Procrastination and the priority of short-term mood regulation: consequences for future self. *Social and Personality Psychology Compass*, 7(2), 115-127. Conceptual review: procrastination is largely short-term mood repair, with the cost pushed onto the "future self." [White Rose eprint](https://eprints.whiterose.ac.uk/91793/)
- **Self-compassion:** Sirois, F. M. (2014). Procrastination and stress: exploring the role of self-compassion. *Self and Identity*, 13(2), 128-145. Four samples (145, 339 and 190 undergraduates; 94 community adults; total 768). Trait procrastination went with lower self-compassion and higher stress; an internal meta-analysis found a moderate negative procrastination-self-compassion link; self-compassion mediated the procrastination-stress link in all four samples. Cross-sectional. [Text copy](https://is.muni.cz/el/phil/podzim2017/PS_BB001/um/clanky_na_porovnanie/PR_2014_Sirois.txt); [White Rose eprint](https://eprints.whiterose.ac.uk/91791/)
- **Self-forgiveness:** Wohl, M. J. A., Pychyl, T. A. & Bennett, S. H. (2010). I forgive myself, now I can study: how self-forgiveness for procrastinating can reduce future procrastination. *Personality and Individual Differences*, 48(7), 803-808. First-year students (134 recruited; 119 analyzed). Forgiving oneself for procrastinating before the first midterm predicted less procrastination before the second, through less negative affect; the link held only among heavy procrastinators. Observational, not a trial. [BPS Research Digest](https://bps.org.uk/research-digest/cure-procrastination-forgive-yourself); [summary with sample details](https://www.spring.org.uk/2010/05/procrastinate-less-by-forgiving-yourself.php)
- **Guilt spoils leisure as recovery:** Reinecke, L., Hartmann, T. & Eden, A. (2014). The guilty couch potato: the role of ego depletion in reducing recovery through media use. *Journal of Communication*. Online survey, n = 471, recall of the previous day. More depleted people were more likely to see their TV or video game use as procrastination; the resulting guilt reduced the recovery benefit. Cross-sectional recall design. [ScienceDaily](https://www.sciencedaily.com/releases/2014/07/140729073636.htm); [EurekAlert](https://www.eurekalert.org/news-releases/511380)
- **Breaks (meta-analysis):** Albulescu, P., Macsinga, I., Rusu, A., Sulea, C., Bodnaru, A. & Tulbure, B. T. (2022). "Give me a break!" A systematic review and meta-analysis on the efficacy of micro-breaks for increasing well-being and performance. *PLoS ONE*, 17(8), e0272460. 22 samples, N = 2,335; breaks of 10 minutes or less. Vigor d = .36 (k = 9, n = 913), fatigue reduction d = .35 (k = 9, n = 803), performance d = .16, not significant (k = 15, n = 1,132); performance gains only for less cognitively demanding tasks; longer breaks predicted larger performance gains. [PMC9432722](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC9432722/); [PubMed 36044424](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/36044424/)
- **Deadlines as structure:** Ariely, D. & Wertenbroch, K. (2002). Procrastination, deadlines, and performance: self-control by precommitment. *Psychological Science*, 13(3), 219-224. People voluntarily set costly deadlines for themselves; self-set deadlines helped but less than evenly spaced external deadlines; people did not set them optimally. [PubMed 12009041](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/12009041/). Challenges: a later replication of Study 2 did not reproduce the result ([Psychological Science](https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/full/10.1177/09567976261460772), match to this replication taken from search summary), and Bisin & Hyndman found deadlines did not raise task completion ([NBER w19874](https://www.nber.org/system/files/working_papers/w19874/revisions/w19874.rev0.pdf)).

### Inferences
- Guilt after procrastinating is part of the problem, not the cure: it lowers mood, and procrastination is mood repair, so guilt makes the next avoidance more likely. Self-forgiveness and self-compassion predict the reverse. This supports the app's no-shame voice.
- A guilt-free place to procrastinate is supported indirectly: Reinecke shows guilt removes the recovery value of games and TV. The evidence does not show that games then lead people back to work.
- Breaks restore energy but not performance on hard tasks; the benefit needs an end point. The app's "breaks with an end" fits this. Chess, which is cognitively demanding, may restore less than a lighter game (inference only).
- Temporal motivation theory explains "panic works": as delay shrinks, motivation rises whether or not fear is present. Smaller, closer self-chosen deadlines can produce that effect without the panic.

### Gaps
- Exact Steel (2007) correlations (often quoted as about -.62 for conscientiousness, .41 for impulsiveness, -.38 for self-efficacy) could not be verified this session.
- No empirical study of Perry's "structured procrastination" found.
- No randomized trial found of self-compassion or self-forgiveness interventions for procrastination in ADHD; the Beaton trial (NCT04978792) targets criticism and mental health and has no results found.
- Sirois, Molnar & Hirsch (2017) perfectionism-procrastination meta-analysis was seen in results but its numbers were not retrieved.

## 8. Parenting: harsh, guilt-inducing or psychologically controlling parenting vs warm structure, ADHD outcomes, and parent training based on positive reinforcement

### Takeaway
Guilt- and approval-based parenting (conditional regard) does get children to comply, but through internal compulsion, with anxiety, shame, unstable self-esteem and resentment. Harsh, negative parenting predicts worse later ADHD outcomes (in both directions), and warmth predicts better ones. Behavioral parent training built mainly on praise and positive reinforcement reliably improves parenting and conduct, though blinded ratings do not show it reduces core ADHD symptoms. Evidence strength: Moderate (meta-analyses, mostly child samples and observational for parenting style; Assor's work includes college students).

### Cited Findings
- **Conditional regard (includes college students):** Assor, A., Roth, G. & Deci, E. L. (2004). The emotional costs of parents' conditional regard: a self-determination theory analysis. *Journal of Personality*, 72(1), 47-88. Study 1: three generations; Study 2: college students across four domains (emotion control, prosocial, academic, sport). Conditional regard "can promote enactment of the desired behaviors but does so with significant affective costs": it predicted introjected internalization (felt internal compulsion), resentment toward parents, anxiety before performance, shame and guilt after failure, short-lived satisfaction after success, and fluctuating self-esteem. [PubMed 14686884](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/14686884/); [PDF](https://www.selfdeterminationtheory.org/SDT/documents/2004_AssorRothDeci.pdf)
- Roth, G., Assor, A., Niemiec, C. P., Ryan, R. M. & Deci, E. L. (2009), *Developmental Psychology*: even conditional *positive* regard had adverse emotional and academic consequences relative to autonomy support. [PDF](https://selfdeterminationtheory.org/wp-content/uploads/2014/04/2009_RothAssorNiemiecRyanDeci.pdf)
- **Parenting and later ADHD (CHILD, longitudinal meta-analysis):** Claussen, A. H., et al. (2024, online 2022). Factors associated with self-regulation and ADHD: a systematic review and meta-analysis of longitudinal studies. *Prevention Science*, 25, 249-271. 52 longitudinal studies (plus 7 in a 2021 update). Negativity/harsh discipline, intrusiveness, maltreatment and family factors had significant direct associations with later ADHD outcomes; sensitivity/warmth had an inverse association; heterogeneity was significant. [PMC9017071](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC9017071); [PubMed 35438451](https://www.pubmed.gov/35438451/)
- **UK cohort (CHILD):** among 5- and 7-year-olds with ADHD symptoms, harsh or withdrawn parenting strengthened the link between ADHD symptoms and later peer problems; withdrawn parenting was linked to emotion dysregulation. [UCL Centre for Longitudinal Studies](https://cls.ucl.ac.uk/parenting-practices-and-adhd/)
- **Behavioral parent training (CHILD, meta-analysis):** Daley, D., van der Oord, S., Ferrin, M., et al. (2014). Behavioral interventions in ADHD: a meta-analysis of randomized controlled trials across multiple outcome domains. *JAACAP*, 53(8), 835-847. With unblinded raters, gains across parenting, ADHD symptoms, conduct, social skills and academics. With probably blinded raters, effects held for positive parenting (SMD about 0.63), less negative parenting (about 0.43) and conduct problems (about 0.31), but not core ADHD symptoms. [PubMed 25062591](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/25062591/)
- A 2023 meta-analysis of longer-term outcomes found sustained improvements from behavioral parent training in child ADHD symptoms and behavior problems. [PMC10501699](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC10501699/)

### Inferences
- Marcelo's account of his and his peers' upbringing (strictness plus guilt) matches conditional regard almost exactly, and the Assor findings match his "works but unhealthy" summary: compliance through internal compulsion, with anxiety, shame and resentment.
- The effective alternative is not permissiveness: warm structure with positive reinforcement (parent training) improves behavior and parenting, and warmth predicts better ADHD outcomes. The adult equivalent is clear structure delivered kindly, with praise for starts.
- Resentment toward the controlling source (Assor) is a plausible root of his urge to break out of strict structures later (section 10).

### Gaps
- Most parenting and ADHD evidence is from children; no study found linking remembered guilt-based parenting to adult ADHD outcomes or to adult self-motivation styles in ADHD.
- A meta-analysis specifically on psychological control (as distinct from harsh discipline) and ADHD was not found.

## 9. Growth mindset: how strong is the effect really? (Sisk et al. 2018; Burnette et al. 2023; Macnamara & Burgoyne 2023; Yeager et al. 2019)

### Takeaway
Growth mindset interventions have small average effects on grades (about d = 0.05 to 0.14 depending on the meta-analysis and method), possibly near zero in the best-designed studies, with somewhat larger effects for struggling students in supportive settings and possibly for mental health (d = 0.32). It is a weak lever on its own. Also, what Marcelo calls "growth mindset" (chosen goals that build capability) is closer to autonomous or identified motivation (section 3), which has a stronger evidence base. Evidence strength: Strong that average academic effects are small; contested whether subgroup effects are real.

### Cited Findings
- Sisk, V. F., Burgoyne, A. P., Sun, J., Butler, J. L. & Macnamara, B. N. (2018). To what extent and under which circumstances are growth mind-sets important to academic achievement? Two meta-analyses. *Psychological Science*, 29(4), 549-571. Meta-analysis 1: mindset and achievement, 129 studies (273 effect sizes), N = 365,915. Meta-analysis 2: interventions, 43 effect sizes, N = 57,155. "Overall effects were weak for both," with some support for benefits among low-SES or academically at-risk students. (The full paper reports roughly r = .10 and d = .08; not re-verified this session.) [PDF](https://englelab.gatech.edu/articles/2018/Sisk,%20Burgoyne%20et%20al.%20(2018)%20-%20Mindset%20and%20Academic%20Achievement.pdf)
- Macnamara, B. N. & Burgoyne, A. P. (2023). Do growth mindset interventions impact students' academic achievement? A systematic review and meta-analysis with recommendations for best practices. *Psychological Bulletin*, 149(3-4), 133-173. 63 studies: d = 0.05; studies that actually changed mindsets: d = 0.04; six high-quality studies: d = 0.02; not significant after publication-bias correction; flags design and reporting problems. [NYU IFP abstract](https://ifp.nyu.edu/?p=1678671); [PDF](https://Www.Gwern.net/doc/statistics/meta-analysis/2023-macnamara.pdf)
- Burnette, J. L., Billingsley, J., Banks, G. C., et al. (2023). A systematic review and meta-analysis of growth mindset interventions: for whom, how, and why might such interventions work? *Psychological Bulletin*, 149(3-4), 174-205. Academic achievement d = 0.14 (95% prediction interval about -0.08 to 0.35); mental health d = 0.32 (prediction interval about 0.07 to 0.57); high-fidelity studies showed d = 0.14 for focal groups vs 0.04 for non-focal groups (secondary summary). [NYU IFP abstract](https://ifp.nyu.edu/2023/meta-analyses-systematic-reviews/bul0000368/)
- Critics argue Burnette's subgroup effects may be inflated because subgroups were sometimes defined after the fact; a commentary reports that reanalyzing Macnamara and Burgoyne's data with heterogeneity-focused methods supported Burnette's conclusions. [Purdue PDF](https://hhs.purdue.edu/skill-learning-and-performance-lab/wp-content/uploads/sites/43/2024/08/2023-90931-004.pdf); [PMC10495100](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10495100/)
- Yeager, D. S., Hanselman, P., Walton, G. M., et al. (2019). A national experiment reveals where a growth mindset improves achievement. *Nature*, 573, 364-369. Nationally representative US 9th graders; a short online intervention improved grades among lower-achieving students (reported as about 0.10 GPA points on a 4-point scale) and raised advanced math enrollment; effects were largest where peer norms supported challenge. Yeager's team estimated a 5.3 percentage point drop in students off track for graduation for those starting near a 2.0 GPA. [PMC6786290](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6786290); [Education Week](https://www.edweek.org/leadership/national-study-bolsters-case-for-teaching-growth-mindset/2019/08)

### Inferences
- Telling people "abilities grow" moves grades very little. Building the app around a "growth mindset" message would add words for little gain.
- The mental health effect (d = 0.32, Burnette) is more promising for a low-guilt app, but it rests on the more optimistic meta-analysis.
- What Marcelo means (choosing goals that build capability, a "why" for each) is better grounded in SDT's identified regulation and approach goals than in mindset research. Naming matters when he presents this.

### Gaps
- No growth mindset study in ADHD samples found. Yeager et al.'s exact n and the subgroup to which 0.10 GPA points applies were not confirmed (the PMC text could not be opened).

## 10. Why do people abandon strict structures? Reactance, rigidity and dropout from self-imposed regimes

### Takeaway
Three lines of research fit Marcelo's "it works, then I want to step out of it": reactance (perceived threats to freedom trigger anger and pushback), the "what-the-hell" or abstinence-violation effect (one lapse from a rigid rule leads to giving up the rule), and the instability of guilt-based motivation (sections 3 and 8). Structure itself is not the enemy: deadlines and external structure do help, but rigid, all-or-nothing, imposed-feeling structure invites collapse. Evidence strength: Moderate for reactance and rigid-restraint effects in general populations; no direct ADHD evidence found.

### Cited Findings
- Rains, S. A. (2013). The nature of psychological reactance revisited: a meta-analytic review. *Human Communication Research*, 39(1), 47-73. 20 studies, about 4,942 participants; reactance is best modeled as intertwined anger and negative thoughts, and this state drives its undesirable behavioral effects. [Oxford Academic](https://academic.oup.com/hcr/article-abstract/39/1/47/4093682)
- **Rigid rules and the what-the-hell effect:** Herman and Polivy's preload studies showed restrained eaters overeat after a forced violation of their diet; their boundary model explains this by strict cognitive limits; stress and comparison with others also trigger it; the same pattern with alcohol and cigarettes is called the abstinence violation effect. Polivy & Herman (2020) note disinhibition is defined relative to a person's own violated standards. [Polivy & Herman 2020, Frontiers in Nutrition](https://www.frontiersin.org/articles/10.3389/fnut.2020.00030/pdf)
- **Structure does help:** self-set deadlines reduced procrastination in Ariely & Wertenbroch (2002), but less than external, evenly spaced deadlines, and later work questions the effect (section 7). [PubMed 12009041](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/12009041/)
- **Controlled motivation has costs:** external regulation has limited links to behavior and costs well-being (Van den Broeck et al. 2021), and conditional regard produces resentment toward its source (Assor et al. 2004). [Monash record](https://research.monash.edu/en/publications/beyond-intrinsic-and-extrinsic-motivation-a-meta-analysis-on-self/); [PubMed 14686884](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/14686884/)

### Inferences
- Even a structure he set himself can feel like an outside rule once it is strict and fixed, which can trigger reactance (anger at the rule) and, after the first miss, a what-the-hell collapse. That fits "it works, then I want to step out of it."
- Design choices this suggests: structure he chooses and can change; lapses that cost nothing and are easy to repair; no streaks to break (the app already avoids them); small fixed endpoints (breaks with an end) rather than all-day regimes.
- Note for the Follower: changing the game's difficulty is less controlling than blocking or lecturing, so it should trigger less reactance. This is untested (inference only).

### Gaps
- No study found on dropout from self-imposed productivity regimes in ADHD, or on reactance in ADHD adults (ADHD is sometimes linked to novelty-seeking and boredom, which was not searched).
- No longitudinal evidence retrieved that introjected motivation fades faster than identified motivation (likely in SDT exercise and health studies, for example Teixeira et al. 2012, not retrieved).

## 11. Synthesis: how Marcelo's five beliefs hold up against the evidence

### Takeaway
The evidence mostly **supports** beliefs 2, 3 and 5 (guilt-based upbringing works through compulsion at a cost; self-chosen valued goals are a healthier engine; guilt-free downtime and self-forgiveness help). It **challenges** belief 1 as a causal story (trauma is a risk, not a recipe; what he sees is survivorship) and **refines** belief 3 (fear does work a little; the healthy replacement is valued goals plus kind structure, not "growth mindset" messages and not less structure). Belief 4 fits reactance and rigid-rule research but has no direct ADHD test.

### Cited Findings
- **Belief 1 (successful ADHD peers are traumatized and fear-driven; fear works):** partly supported, mostly challenged. Fear and guilt do buy persistence (introjection is positively related to persistence: [Howard et al. 2021](https://acuresearchbank.acu.edu.au/item/8w8z7/student-motivation-and-associated-outcomes-a-meta-analysis-from-self-determination-theory); fear appeals d = 0.29: [Tannenbaum et al. 2015](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/26501228/)). But on average adversity goes with more ADHD (OR 1.68: [Zhang & Gao 2022](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC9575611/)) and more PTSD (RR 2.9: [Spencer et al. 2016](https://www.psychiatrist.com/wp-content/uploads/2021/02/13429_examining-association-between-posttraumatic-stress.pdf)); only *some* (not high) adversity is associated with resilience ([Seery et al. 2010](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/20939649/)); the only "successful adults with ADHD" study had 6 men and found strengths, not fear ([Sedgwick et al. 2019](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s12402-018-0277-6)). Strength: Moderate against the causal reading.
- **Belief 2 (parents taught rule-following through strictness and guilt):** supported as a description of a known pattern. Conditional regard yields compliance via internal compulsion, with anxiety, shame, guilt, short-lived satisfaction and resentment ([Assor et al. 2004](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/14686884/)); harsh parenting predicts worse later ADHD outcomes, warmth better ([Claussen et al. 2024](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC9017071)); positive-reinforcement parent training improves parenting and conduct in blinded ratings ([Daley et al. 2014](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/25062591/)). Strength: Moderate; mostly child data.
- **Belief 3 (negative incentive works but is unhealthy; replace with self-chosen goals that grow capability):** supported with refinements. Introjection: persistence plus ill-being; identified regulation: best for persistence and performance; intrinsic: best for well-being ([Howard et al. 2021](https://acuresearchbank.acu.edu.au/item/8w8z7/student-motivation-and-associated-outcomes-a-meta-analysis-from-self-determination-theory); [Van den Broeck et al. 2021](https://research.monash.edu/en/publications/beyond-intrinsic-and-extrinsic-motivation-a-meta-analysis-on-self/)); avoidance goals harm well-being ([Elliot & Sheldon 1997](https://worlddatabaseofhappiness.eur.nl/publications/avoidance-achievement-motivation-a-personal-goals-analysis-10188/)); fear-of-mistakes perfectionism predicts burnout ([Hill & Curran 2016](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/26231736/)). Challenge: growth mindset interventions have small academic effects ([Macnamara & Burgoyne 2023](https://ifp.nyu.edu/?p=1678671); [Burnette et al. 2023](https://ifp.nyu.edu/2023/meta-analyses-systematic-reviews/bul0000368/)), and the SDT evidence base for ADHD is thin ([PMC9066661](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC9066661/)). Strength: Strong in general samples, Weak for ADHD.
- **Belief 4 (strict structure works, then he wants to quit it):** consistent with reactance ([Rains 2013](https://academic.oup.com/hcr/article-abstract/39/1/47/4093682)), the what-the-hell effect after rigid rules ([Polivy & Herman 2020](https://www.frontiersin.org/articles/10.3389/fnut.2020.00030/pdf)), and resentment from controlled motivation ([Assor et al. 2004](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/14686884/)). Challenge: deadlines and structure do help somewhat ([Ariely & Wertenbroch 2002](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/12009041/)), and autonomy without support can be a "double-edged sword" in ADHD ([Frontiers 2025](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/psychology/articles/10.3389/fpsyg.2025.1624279/full)). Strength: Weak to Moderate, indirect.
- **Belief 5 (a guilt-free place to procrastinate, with gentle follow-back):** the "guilt-free" half is supported: guilt erases the recovery value of games and TV ([Reinecke et al. 2014](https://www.sciencedaily.com/releases/2014/07/140729073636.htm)); self-forgiveness predicts less later procrastination ([Wohl et al. 2010](https://bps.org.uk/research-digest/cure-procrastination-forgive-yourself)); self-compassion goes with less procrastination and stress ([Sirois 2014](https://eprints.whiterose.ac.uk/91791/)), and adults with ADHD have less of it ([Beaton et al. 2020](https://eprints.whiterose.ac.uk/165039/)); short breaks restore vigor and reduce fatigue ([Albulescu et al. 2022](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC9432722/)). Challenge: procrastination is mood repair ([Sirois & Pychyl 2013](https://eprints.whiterose.ac.uk/91793/)), so a pleasant game can become the avoidance itself; breaks do not reliably raise performance on demanding tasks ([Albulescu et al. 2022](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC9432722/)). Strength: Moderate for "remove guilt"; no direct evidence for games as a bridge back to work.

### Inferences
- The most defensible one-line position for the app: **keep the structure, remove the fear.** Valued goals (the "why"), small approach-framed first steps, lapses that are always reparable, breaks with an end, and no guilt about play. Each of these has at least moderate general-population support; none has been tested as a package in ADHD.
- His own test data (start delay, play minutes, share of play sessions followed by a start within 10 minutes, response to nudges) would be new evidence on the untested part (belief 5's "follow-back"), and is worth keeping.
- Being honest with him: fear is not useless. It moves behavior a little, especially for single acts with a clear action. The case against it is about cost (anxiety, burnout, shame, resentment, collapse after lapses), not about zero effect.

### Gaps
- No study found that tests, in adults with ADHD, a switch from fear-based to autonomous motivation and measures work output and well-being over time.
- No study found on in-app games as planned procrastination for ADHD, or on whether adaptive game difficulty (his Follower) nudges people back to work.
- Full-text checks were blocked for PubMed Central; exact effect sizes for Kessler (2006) anxiety comorbidity, Steel (2007), Howard (2021) and Yeager (2019) should be verified before quoting precise numbers.
