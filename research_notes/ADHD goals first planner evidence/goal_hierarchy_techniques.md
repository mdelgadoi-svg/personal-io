# Goal hierarchies (long-term, mid-term, weekly, daily) and the planning techniques in the proposed ADHD planner

Scope note: these notes cover the evidence behind each step of Marcelo's proposed process (long-term goals with AI help, mid-term "semi-goals", AI-suggested short-term goals, a life balance scorecard, weekly goals, Eisenhower-style sorting, weekly accountability). Evidence strength labels used throughout: **Strong** (several meta-analyses or many replicated experiments), **Moderate** (a few good experiments or one meta-analysis with caveats), **Weak** (single studies, quasi-experiments, correlational only), **None found** (popular tool or book with no research located). Where a fact was seen only in a secondary summary (search snippet, press release, later paper) and not in the original article, it is marked "secondary". Two full-text hosts (home.ubalt.edu and goal-lab.psych.umn.edu, both hosting Locke & Latham 2002) could not be reached from this session because they are outside the session's allowed network domains, so exact passages from that article come from search summaries.

## 1. Goal-setting theory: specific, difficult goals, and when learning goals beat outcome goals (is a job search a complex task where outcome goals backfire?)

### Takeaway
Specific, difficult goals beat "do your best" on average (meta-analytic d roughly .42 to .82), and this is one of the best-replicated findings in applied psychology (**Strong**). But the benefit shrinks as tasks get more complex, and on new, complex tasks where the person does not yet know the strategy, a specific hard *outcome* goal can do worse than "do your best", while a specific hard *learning* goal does best (**Moderate**: a handful of lab experiments with 96 to 114 students, plus one small job-seeker field experiment). A job search has the marks of a complex, novel task (many parts, other people decide the outcome, the strategy has to be discovered), so weekly goals should be behaviors and learning goals he controls, not outcomes.

### Cited Findings
- Locke & Latham (2002), "Building a practically useful theory of goal setting and task motivation: A 35-year odyssey," *American Psychologist*, 57(9), 705-717. The article reports meta-analytic effect sizes for goal difficulty of d = .52 to .82, and for specific difficult goals versus "do your best" of d = .42 to .80 (both citing Locke & Latham, 1990). These are two different comparisons and should not be merged (secondary: search summary of the article text). ([Locke & Latham 2002 PDF](https://goal-lab.psych.umn.edu/orgpsych/2020/readings/5.%20Motivation/Locke%20&%20Latham%20(2002).pdf); [bibliographic record](https://bibbase.org/network/publication/locke-latham-buildingapracticallyusefultheoryofgoalsettingandtaskmotivationa35yearodyssey-2002))
- Mento, Steel & Karren (1987), *Organizational Behavior and Human Decision Processes*, 39(1), 52-83, a meta-analysis of 1966-1984 studies: "strong support" for the goal difficulty and goal specificity/difficulty parts of the theory; lab versus field setting moderated the specificity/difficulty effect. ([EconPapers record](https://econpapers.repec.org/RePEc:eee:jobhdp:v:39:y:1987:i:1:p:52-83))
- A secondary document reports Mento et al.'s goal-difficulty effect as d = .581 (versus .816 in Tubbs, 1986). Not checked against the original. ([Scribd summary, low authority](https://www.scribd.com/document/182517000/Goal-Diffulty-3))
- Wood, Mento & Locke (1987), "Task complexity as a moderator of goal effects: A meta-analysis," *Journal of Applied Psychology*: 125 studies from 1966-1985; task complexity rated on component, coordinative and dynamic complexity (rating reliability .92); "task complexity was found to be a significant moderator" of the effects of goal difficulty and goal specificity-difficulty (effects weaker on complex tasks). A secondary source says complexity explained 5.7% of the variance in effect sizes (not verified). ([Zenodo full text record](https://zenodo.org/records/1258559); [Scribd summary](https://www.scribd.com/document/182517000/Goal-Diffulty-3))
- Winters & Latham (1996), "The effect of learning versus outcome goals on a simple versus a complex task," *Group & Organization Management*, 21(2), 236-250; n = 114 business students, scheduling task. Simple task: an outcome goal produced more correct schedules than "do your best". Complex task: a learning goal produced higher performance than either an outcome goal or "do your best", and more effective strategies. Authors recommend specific difficult learning goals, not outcome goals, "when attempting new complex processes." ([Record and abstract](https://bvbvf1.bib-bvb.de/vufind/PrimoRecord/cdi_proquest_journals_203376963))
- Seijts & Latham (2001), "The effect of distal learning, outcome, and proximal goals on a moderately complex task," *Journal of Organizational Behavior*, 22, 291-307; n = 96, class-scheduling task that required acquiring knowledge. A "do your best" outcome goal produced *higher* performance than a specific, difficult outcome goal; a specific, difficult learning goal beat "do your best"; commitment was higher under learning goals; self-efficacy's link to performance ran through strategy development. ([Zendy record](https://zendy.io/title/10.1002/job.70); [Scinapse record](https://www.scinapse.io/papers/2019557603))
- Seijts & Latham (2005), "Learning versus performance goals: When should each be used?" (*Academy of Management Executive*), a practitioner summary of this line of work. ([PDF](https://wku.edu/cebs/doctorate/documents/readings/seijts_latham_2005_learning_vs_performance_goals.pdf))
- Job search specifically: van Hooft & Noordzij (2009), "The effects of goal orientation on job search and reemployment: A field experiment among unemployed job seekers," *Journal of Applied Psychology*, 94(6), 1581-1590; 109 unemployed job seekers in the Netherlands randomly assigned to a learning-goal workshop, a performance-goal workshop, or a control workshop, measuring job search intentions, search behavior and reemployment; effects appeared independent of dispositional goal orientation. ([Erasmus repository](https://pure.eur.nl/en/publications/the-effects-of-goal-orientation-on-job-search-and-reemployment-a-/))
- The US Department of Labor CLEAR review of that study rates the causal evidence as low for some comparisons and notes high attrition. ([CLEAR review](https://clear.dol.gov/study/effects-goal-orientation-job-search-and-reemployment-field-experiment-among-unemployed-job))
- Follow-up: Noordzij et al. (2013), *Personnel Psychology*, a quasi-experimental field study of a learning-goal training with 223 unemployed job seekers. ([Erasmus repository](https://repub.eur.nl/pub/40905))
- Liu, Huang & Wang (2014), "Effectiveness of job search interventions: A meta-analytic review," *Psychological Bulletin*, 140(4), 1009-1041: 47 experimental or quasi-experimental studies, 9,575 job seekers; odds of employment 2.67 times higher with a job search intervention; components associated with better outcomes included teaching job search skills, improving self-presentation, boosting self-efficacy, encouraging proactivity, and promoting goal setting; interventions worked only when they combined skill development with motivation enhancement. ([Monash record](https://research.monash.edu/en/publications/effectiveness-of-job-search-interventions-a-meta-analytic-review/); [ScienceDaily](https://www.sciencedaily.com/releases/2014/05/140512124110.htm))
- Press coverage of Liu et al. gives component odds of 3.40 for self-presentation training and 3.32 for job search skills (secondary). ([Phys.org](https://phys.org/news/2014-05-key-success-job.html))

### Inferences
- A Bay Area job search in behavioral economics or climate adaptation investing scores high on all three complexity dimensions in Wood et al. (1987): many components (courses, networking, project, applications), coordination between them, and dynamic change (hiring cycles, other people's replies). For him it is also partly novel (new sector entry), which is exactly the condition in Winters & Latham (1996) and Seijts & Latham (2001) where hard outcome goals underperformed.
- So: keep "get a job in the Bay Area ... start within a year, keep for 3 years" as the distal direction, but do not turn it into weekly outcome targets ("get 3 interviews this week"). Weekly goals should be (a) behaviors fully in his control ("10 outreach messages", "homework on time") and (b) one learning goal ("find out which 5 Bay Area funds do climate adaptation and what their analysts did before", "test two message versions and see which gets replies").
- "10 outreach messages" is a good goal by this evidence: specific, hard, and controllable. "Aspirational but achievable" matches "difficult but attainable", but the evidence says the "difficult" part should sit on effort and learning, not on outcomes others control.
- Evidence strength: main effect **Strong**; learning-versus-outcome moderator **Moderate** (small lab samples, replicated by the same group); job-search-specific evidence for learning goals **Weak** (one small field experiment with high attrition); job search interventions with goal setting as a component **Strong** (meta-analysis), though goal setting's separate contribution is not isolated.

### Gaps
- Exact simple-versus-complex effect sizes from Wood, Mento & Locke (1987) were not found in accessible text.
- The direction and size of van Hooft & Noordzij's (2009) results were not visible in the search snippets (design only). From memory of the abstract, the learning-goal workshop raised search intentions, behavior and reemployment relative to control; this was not verified in this session.
- Locke & Latham (2019), "The development of goal setting theory: A half century retrospective," *Motivation Science*, was not retrieved.
- Locke & Latham's statements that performance rises with goal difficulty until ability limits, and that commitment drops when goals seem impossible, could not be checked because the full text hosts were blocked.
- The separate odds ratio for the goal-setting component in Liu et al. (2014) was not found.

## 2. Proximal versus distal goals: do subgoals improve persistence and self-efficacy?

### Takeaway
Yes, with caveats. Classic experiments show proximal subgoals produce faster progress, more mastery, higher self-efficacy and more interest than a distal goal alone (**Moderate**: small experiments, mostly children, replicated conceptually). In adult complex-task studies, adding proximal goals helped people find strategies but did not always raise performance by itself. For ADHD the case is stronger in theory, because people with ADHD discount delayed rewards more steeply (meta-analytic d = .43), so a distant goal pulls less.

### Cited Findings
- Bandura & Schunk (1981), "Cultivating competence, self-efficacy, and intrinsic interest through proximal self-motivation," *Journal of Personality and Social Psychology*, 41(3), 586-598. Children with arithmetic deficits pursued a self-directed program under proximal subgoals, a distal goal, or no goal reference. Under proximal subgoals they "progressed rapidly in self-directed learning, achieved substantial mastery of mathematical operations, and developed a sense of personal efficacy and intrinsic interest in arithmetic activities that initially held little appeal for them." ([Full text PDF](https://uploads-ssl.webflow.com/59faaf5b01b9500001e95457/5bc552d85141987915dab842_Bandura%20&%20Schunk,%201981.pdf))
- Stock & Cervone (1990), *Cognitive Therapy and Research* (DOI 10.1007/BF01172969): setting a subgoal raised initial self-efficacy; reaching the proximal goal raised self-efficacy, self-satisfaction and later persistence (secondary: search summary). ([DOI link](https://dx.doi.org/10.1007/BF01172969))
- Schunk (1985), participation in goal setting with learning-disabled children: proximal goals promoted motivation more than no goals, and children who took part in setting their own goals had the highest self-efficacy and subtraction skill (secondary: search summary). ([Typeset record](https://typeset.io/pdf/participation-in-goal-setting-effects-on-self-efficacy-and-3r9necwxog.pdf); [ERIC related report](https://files.eric.ed.gov/fulltext/ED199284.pdf))
- Seijts & Latham (2001): participants with a distal learning goal plus proximal goals discovered more task-relevant strategies than any other condition, but adding proximal outcome goals to a distal goal did not raise performance over the distal goal alone. ([Zendy record](https://zendy.io/title/10.1002/job.70); [Scinapse record](https://www.scinapse.io/papers/2019557603))
- ADHD: Jackson & MacKillop (2016), "Attention-deficit/hyperactivity disorder and monetary delay discounting: A meta-analysis of case-control studies," *Biological Psychiatry: Cognitive Neuroscience and Neuroimaging*, 1(4), 316-325: 21 investigations, 25 case-control comparisons, N = 3,913; people with ADHD discount delayed rewards more steeply, d = 0.43 (p < 10^-15); no evidence of publication bias. ([PubMed](https://www.pubmed.gov/27722208/); [PMC full text](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC5049699))
- Age split reported by a later review: d = 0.47 under 18 and d = 0.40 over 18 (secondary, not checked). ([PMC review](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC7912943/))

### Inferences
- His structure (years, then under a year, then weeks, then days) is a proximal-goal hierarchy, which is supported. The weekly and daily levels do the motivational work; the long-term level supplies meaning.
- The adult complex-task result suggests proximal goals help most when they are learning or behavior steps that reveal strategy, not mini outcome quotas.
- Schunk's participation finding (in learning-disabled children) supports letting him set or edit the subgoals rather than receiving them from the AI.
- For ADHD, steeper discounting means rewards a year away weigh little today; weekly and daily goals with visible completion are likely more important for him than for a typical user. This is an inference from discounting research, not a tested ADHD goal-hierarchy trial.

### Gaps
- Exact sample size and statistics of Bandura & Schunk (1981) were not extracted (recalled as about 40 children; not verified).
- Latham & Seijts (1999), *Journal of Organizational Behavior*, on proximal plus distal goals in a moderately complex profit task, was not retrieved.
- No meta-analysis specifically comparing proximal and distal goals in adults was found.
- No study testing goal hierarchies (years, months, weeks) in adults with ADHD was found.

## 3. Goal self-concordance: do self-chosen, value-aligned goals get more sustained effort and attainment?

### Takeaway
Yes. Goals pursued for autonomous reasons (interest, personal value) predict more goal progress (meta-analytic d about .37 to .42), largely through sustained effort; goals pursued for controlled reasons (pressure, guilt, others' expectations) show essentially no relation to progress (d about -.02) (**Moderate to Strong** for the association, from many longitudinal studies; weaker for interventions that try to raise self-concordance). This directly bears on AI-suggested goals.

### Cited Findings
- Sheldon & Elliot (1999), "Goal striving, need satisfaction, and longitudinal well-being: The self-concordance model," *Journal of Personality and Social Psychology*, 76(3), 482-497: the model follows the sequence from goal choice to attainment; self-concordant goals get more sustained effort, which is the route to attainment, which then predicts need satisfaction and well-being (secondary summaries). ([Seger et al. 2025, Motivation Science](https://www.southampton.ac.uk/~crsi/Seger%20et%20al.,%202025,%20Motivation%20Science.pdf); [Wikiversity summary](https://en.wikiversity.org/wiki/Motivation_and_emotion/Book/2018/Self-concordance_theory_and_motivation))
- Sheldon & Houser-Marko (2001), "Self-concordance, goal attainment, and the pursuit of happiness: Can there be an upward spiral?" *Journal of Personality and Social Psychology*, 80, 152-165: in first-year students, self-concordance predicted attainment, attainment predicted better adjustment and more self-concordant later goals, and that increased self-concordance predicted even better attainment in the second semester (secondary). ([Journal index record](https://acnpsearch.tweb-dev.unibo.it/singlejournalindex/78713115); [Psychology Today summary](https://www.psychologytoday.com/us/blog/dont-delay/200806/getting-off-to-a-good-start-an-upward-spiral-of-happiness))
- Koestner, Lekes, Powers & Chicoine (2002), *Journal of Personality and Social Psychology*: a meta-analysis of the 7 published studies on self-concordance and goal progress gave d = 0.37. ([Full text PDF](https://www.selfdeterminationtheory.org/SDT/documents/2002_KoestnerLekesPowersChicoine_JPSP.pdf))
- Koestner, Otis, Powers, Pelletier & Gagnon (2008), "Autonomous motivation, controlled motivation, and goal progress," *Journal of Personality*: three studies plus a meta-analysis; autonomous motivation was substantially related to goal progress, controlled motivation was not; the link from autonomous motivation to progress involved implementation planning; authors advise focusing on raising autonomous motivation rather than reducing controlled motivation. A later paper reports the meta-analytic values as d = .42 (autonomous) and d = -.02 (controlled) (secondary). ([Loughborough record](https://vufind.lboro.ac.uk/PrimoRecord/cdi_proquest_miscellaneous_69592016); [Koestner 2008 review, Canadian Psychology](https://selfdeterminationtheory.org/wp-content/uploads/2014/04/2008_Koestner_CanPsyc.pdf.pdf))
- Sezer et al. (2024), *Motivation Science*: a systematic review and meta-analysis testing a version of the self-concordance model that splits autonomous and controlled motives (results not extracted here). ([Southampton repository](https://eprints.soton.ac.uk/493985))
- Related: autonomy-supportive versus directive goal support from others affects goal internalization and persistence (Carleton Goal Lab papers; results not extracted). ([Carleton PDF](https://carleton.ca/goallab/wp-content/uploads/Goal-Internalization-and-Persistence-as-a-Function-of-Autonomous-and-Directive-Forms-of-Goal-Support.pdf))

### Inferences
- His step "long-term goals decided by the person with AI help" fits the evidence. The risk is step 2c: short-term goals *suggested by an AI agent* from email and calendar. Email is full of other people's requests, which tend to be controlled motives ("I should answer this"). By the 2008 meta-analysis, such goals would add load without adding progress.
- Practical fix: the AI proposes, he chooses. Every suggested goal needs his own "why" (the app already asks a why for each goal) and a one-tap "not mine" to drop it. The AI should attach email items to his existing goals as tasks, not create new goals from them.
- Treating "the biggest impact fund in Latin America" as a direction, not a goal, is consistent with the model: it is the value source that makes the job goal autonomous. Showing it near the job goal at planning time is reasonable.
- The 2008 finding that autonomy works partly through implementation planning means the "why" and the if-then plan should be paired, not offered as alternatives.

### Gaps
- Exact effect sizes in Sheldon & Elliot (1999) and Sheldon & Houser-Marko (2001) were not verified (search returned secondary summaries).
- Evidence that an intervention can *raise* self-concordance (for example Sheldon et al. 2002 goal training) was not retrieved.
- No ADHD-specific self-concordance research was found.

## 4. Construal level and action identification: "why" thinking, "how" thinking, and pairing a why with a concrete first step

### Takeaway
Thinking about *why* (high-level construal) helps self-control at moments of choice, such as preferring a larger later reward over a smaller immediate one; thinking about *how, when and where* (low-level construal) makes people start sooner. Each helps at a different moment (**Moderate**: multi-study papers by the same research groups; single papers for each specific finding). Pairing a short why (for choosing) with a concrete first step (for starting) is consistent with both.

### Cited Findings
- Fujita, Trope, Liberman & Levin-Sagi (2006), "Construal levels and self-control," *Journal of Personality and Social Psychology*, 90(3), 351-367: six experiments, three different ways of inducing construal level; people induced to think at higher levels were more willing to give up smaller immediate rewards for larger delayed ones; high-level construal highlights global goals and supports self-control, low-level construal highlights local goals. ([PubMed](https://www.pubmed.gov/16594824/); [PMC article discussing it](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3153425))
- McCrea, Liberman, Trope & Sherman (2008), "Construal level and procrastination," *Psychological Science*, 19(12), 1308-1314: participants had 3 weeks to return a questionnaire by e-mail; when the questionnaire induced a concrete construal, they were less likely to procrastinate and responded much sooner; the effect did not depend on the task's attractiveness, importance or perceived difficulty. Note the outcome was speed of returning the questionnaire, not a broad measure of work completion. ([Konstanz full text](https://kops.uni-konstanz.de/entities/publication/42507a45-e0af-45cf-a38e-51b39cb0e5f4/full); [APS journal page](https://www.psychologicalscience.org/journals/psychological-science/j.1467-9280.2008.02240.x/); [ScienceDaily](https://www.sciencedaily.com/releases/2009/01/090112110106.htm))
- Construal-level theory: distant events are represented abstractly, near events concretely; McCrea et al. argue the link runs both ways, so representing a task concretely brings its execution closer in time. ([Konstanz full text](https://kops.uni-konstanz.de/entities/publication/42507a45-e0af-45cf-a38e-51b39cb0e5f4/full))
- Vallacher & Wegner (1987), "What do people think they're doing? Action identification and human behavior," *Psychological Review*, 94(1), 3-15: actions can be identified at high levels (purpose) or low levels (mechanics); people prefer higher-level identities, but shift to lower levels when an action is difficult, unfamiliar or disrupted. (DOI link; this summary is from the paper's well-known abstract and was not opened in this session.) ([DOI](https://doi.org/10.1037/0033-295X.94.1.3))

### Inferences
- The app already pairs each goal with a why and each task with a "first small step". The evidence suggests *when* to show each: show the why at choice points (weekly planning, choosing between a quick urgent email and a job-search message) and show the concrete step at the start moment (the Today card and start screen). Showing the big life goal on the start screen may not help starting and could raise the felt size of the task.
- Action identification theory adds that for a hard or unfamiliar task (writing to a senior investor), people naturally need low-level identities ("open the draft, change the first line"); a first step that is physical and small fits this.
- This supports the 3-level design: the "why" lives in Life Planning, the first step lives in Today.

### Gaps
- No meta-analysis of construal level and self-control or procrastination was located in this session, and the replicability of construal-level priming effects was not checked.
- No ADHD-specific construal-level research was found.

## 5. Implementation intentions (if-then plans), including ADHD studies

### Takeaway
If-then plans ("If it is Monday 9 AM, then I write the first outreach message") are among the best-supported self-regulation tools: d = .65 on goal attainment in the 2006 meta-analysis (94 tests, over 8,000 people) and d = .27 to .66 across outcome types in the 2024 meta-analysis of 642 tests (**Strong**, though newer and larger analyses give smaller effects). They work best in if-then format, when motivation is high, and when rehearsed. Their benefit fades when a person has many goals at once. In children with ADHD, if-then plans brought response inhibition up to the level of children without ADHD in lab tasks (**Weak to Moderate**: small lab studies in children only).

### Cited Findings
- Gollwitzer & Sheeran (2006), "Implementation intentions and goal achievement: A meta-analysis of effects and processes," *Advances in Experimental Social Psychology*, 38, 69-119: 94 independent tests, more than 8,000 participants; d = .65 on goal attainment, mostly against controls who had formed goal intentions; d = .77 on preventing derailment of goal striving (secondary for the participant count and the .77). ([Konstanz full text](https://kops.uni-konstanz.de/server/api/core/bitstreams/eb48c8cf-7855-478e-90ec-1ea6d44f8252/content); [Konstanz publication page](https://www.socmot.uni-konstanz.de/publications/implementation-intentions-and-goal-achievement-meta-analysis-effects-and-processes); [Frontiers review citing it](https://www.frontiersin.org/articles/10.3389/fnhum.2015.00395/full))
- Sheeran, Listrom & Gollwitzer (2024), "The when and how of planning: Meta-analysis of the scope and components of implementation intentions in 642 tests," *European Review of Social Psychology* (DOI 10.1080/10463283.2024.2334563): effective for cognitive, affective and behavioral outcomes (.27 to .66 in d); larger effects when plans had a contingent if-then format, when participants were highly motivated, and when plans were rehearsed; includes a taxonomy of plan contents. ([Taylor & Francis](https://informahealthcare.com/doi/full/10.1080/10463283.2024.2334563?src=exp-la))
- Note on the brief: Wang, Wang & Gai (2021) is a meta-analysis of MCII (mental contrasting plus implementation intentions), not of implementation intentions alone; see section 6.
- Dalton & Spiller (2012), "Too much of a good thing: The benefits of implementation intentions depend on the number of goals," *Journal of Consumer Research*, 39(3), 600-614: specific when-and-where plans helped people with one daily goal but not people with six; planning made the obstacles and constraints of the whole set salient; telling six-task participants that others had even more tasks raised completion. ([IDEAS record](https://ideas.repec.org/a/oup/jconrs/doi10.1086-664500.html); [ScienceDaily](https://sciencedaily.com/releases/2012/05/120516152448.htm); [EurekAlert](https://www.eurekalert.org/news-releases/749027))
- Koestner et al. (2008): the link from autonomous motivation to goal progress ran through implementation planning. ([Loughborough record](https://vufind.lboro.ac.uk/PrimoRecord/cdi_proquest_miscellaneous_69592016))
- ADHD: Gawrilow & Gollwitzer (2008), "Implementation intentions facilitate response inhibition in children with ADHD," *Cognitive Therapy and Research*, 32, 261-280. Study 1 (Go/NoGo): children with ADHD who added an if-then plan to a suppression goal withheld the unwanted response as well as children without ADHD. Study 2: if-then plans plus stimulant medication gave the best suppression. ([Konstanz publication page](https://www.socmot.uni-konstanz.de/publications/implementation-intentions-facilitate-response-inhibition-adhd-children); [PDF](https://www.socmot.uni-konstanz.de/sites/default/files/08_Gawrilow_Gollwitzer_Implementation_intentions_facilit.pdf))
- ADHD: Paul-Jordanov, Bechtold & Gawrilow (2010), *ADHD Attention Deficit and Hyperactivity Disorders*: methylphenidate and if-then plans each improved Go/NoGo inhibition in children with ADHD to the level of children without ADHD. ([Springer](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s12402-010-0028-9))
- ADHD: Gawrilow, Gollwitzer & Oettingen (2011), "If-then plans benefit delay of gratification performance in children with and without ADHD," *Cognitive Therapy and Research*, and a 2011 paper on executive function in ADHD (results not extracted here). ([Delay PDF](https://www.socmot.uni-konstanz.de/sites/default/files/Gawrilow_Gollwitzer_Oettingen_Delay_2011-2.pdf); [Executive function PDF](https://www.socmot.uni-konstanz.de/sites/default/files/Gawrilow_Gollwitzer_Oettingen_Executive_Function_JSCP_2011.pdf))

### Inferences
- Each weekly goal should carry one if-then plan with a real cue from his week ("If I finish my 10 AM class on Tuesday, then I send 2 outreach messages from the library"). The app's planned start times plus reminders are already a form of if-then cue; making the plan explicit in his words adds the "contingent format" that the 2024 meta-analysis links to bigger effects.
- Rehearsal matters (2024 meta-analysis): reading the plan back at the weekly review or when the reminder fires is cheap rehearsal.
- Because of Dalton & Spiller, if-then plans should go on 1 to 3 weekly goals, not on everything.
- The ADHD evidence is encouraging but narrow: children, lab inhibition tasks, short sessions, often the same research group. It shows if-then plans can work in ADHD, not that they improve real-world goal attainment in adults with ADHD.

### Gaps
- Paul, Gawrilow et al. (2007) on if-then planning and the P300 in children with ADHD (NeuroReport) was not retrieved or verified.
- No trials of implementation intentions in adults with ADHD, or on real-world outcomes in ADHD, were found in this session.
- Sample sizes of the Gawrilow studies were not extracted.

## 6. Mental contrasting and MCII / WOOP: are "aspirational" positive goals a problem?

### Takeaway
Positive fantasies alone about a desired future predict *less* effort and worse results, including in a job search: graduates with more positive job fantasies applied to fewer jobs, got fewer offers and earned less two years later (**Weak to Moderate**: correlational, one small sample for the job study, but consistent across four studies and later energy experiments). Mental contrasting with implementation intentions (MCII, sold as WOOP: Wish, Outcome, Obstacle, Plan) improves goal attainment with a small to medium effect (g = 0.34 across 21 studies; g = 0.28 in health behavior), probably smaller after publication bias (**Moderate**). One study found MCII helped schoolchildren at risk for ADHD most (**Weak**: one study, parent ratings). For "aspirational" goals, the evidence says: keep the wish, but always pair it with the obstacle and a plan.

### Cited Findings
- Oettingen & Mayer (2002), "The motivating function of thinking about the future: Expectations versus fantasies," *Journal of Personality and Social Psychology*, 83(5), 1198-1212: four studies (graduates looking for jobs, students with a crush, students before an exam, hip-replacement patients). Positive expectations (judging the future as likely) predicted high effort and success; positive fantasies (vividly imagining the ideal future) predicted the reverse. ([Full text PDF](https://www.psy.uni-hamburg.de/arbeitsbereiche/paedagogische-psychologie-und-motivation/personen/oettingen-gabriele/dokumente/oettingen-mayer-2002.pdf))
- Job study details (secondary, via a summary quoting the New Yorker): about 83 students; two years after graduation, those with more positive job fantasies had sent fewer applications, received fewer offers, and earned lower salaries. ([PsyBlog summary](https://www.spring.org.uk/?p=13256); [Prospective Psychology summary](https://prospectivepsych.org/node/60))
- Kappes & Oettingen (2011), "Positive fantasies about idealized futures sap energy," *Journal of Experimental Social Psychology*, 47(4), 719-729: four studies; induced positive fantasies produced less energy than fantasies that questioned the future, negative fantasies, or neutral fantasies, measured by self-report and systolic blood pressure. ([Full text PDF](https://www.psy.uni-hamburg.de/arbeitsbereiche/paedagogische-psychologie-und-motivation/personen/oettingen-gabriele/dokumente/kappes-oettingen-2011.pdf); [BPS Research Digest](https://bps.org.uk/research-digest/why-positive-fantasies-make-your-dreams-less-likely-come-true))
- Critique: a commentary argues that lower blood pressure may reflect calm rather than missing energy. ([Psychology Today](https://psychologytoday.com/nz/blog/the-new-resilience/201107/does-imagining-goal-make-you-less-likely-achieve-it))
- Wang, Wang & Gai (2021), "A meta-analysis of the effects of mental contrasting with implementation intentions on goal attainment," *Frontiers in Psychology*, 12, 565202: 21 studies, 24 effect sizes, 15,907 participants; overall g = 0.336; interventions delivered through interaction with an experimenter g = 0.465 versus through documents g = 0.277; publication bias suggests true effects may be smaller. ([PMC full text](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC8149892))
- Cross & Sheffield (2019), *Health Psychology Review*: MCII in health behavior g = 0.28 (as reported by Wang et al.). ([PMC full text of Wang et al.](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC8149892))
- ADHD: Gawrilow, Morgenroth, Schultz, Oettingen & Gollwitzer (2013), "Mental contrasting with implementation intentions enhances self-regulation of goal pursuit in schoolchildren at risk for ADHD," *Motivation and Emotion*, 37, 134-145 (online 2012): sixth and seventh graders at risk and not at risk for ADHD; parents rated self-regulation about two weeks later; MCII beat a learning-style intervention, with the largest gains in children at risk for ADHD. ([Springer](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s11031-012-9288-3); [PDF](https://www.socmot.uni-konstanz.de/sites/default/files/Gawrilow_et_al_2012_Mental_contrasting_with_implementation.pdf))

### Inferences
- His phrase "aspirational but achievable" is good if "aspirational" means the direction and "achievable" means he judges it likely. The danger is a planner that invites him to *picture* the dream job (vision boards, inspiring summaries from the AI) without the obstacle step. That is exactly the pattern linked to fewer applications in the job study.
- Add a WOOP step when setting each long-term and 90-day goal: wish (the job), best outcome (one sentence), the inner obstacle ("when I feel I am not senior enough to write to a partner"), and an if-then plan ("then I send the short template and let it go").
- Mental contrasting, by Oettingen's own theory, produces strong commitment only when expectations are high and leads to letting go when they are low. A quick "how likely do you think this is?" check fits here: if he rates a goal unlikely, the app should help him reshape it (smaller scope, longer horizon) rather than push harder.
- The experimenter-guided effect (g = .47) being larger than the document-based one (g = .28) suggests a guided, conversational WOOP (the "Talk it out" or AI agent) may work better than a static form. This is an inference from one moderator.
- For ADHD, the single at-risk-children study is promising, but no adult ADHD MCII trial was found.

### Gaps
- The expectancy-dependence of mental contrasting (Oettingen, Pak & Schnetter 2001, *JPSP*) was not opened in this session; the PDF exists ([PDF](https://www.psy.uni-hamburg.de/arbeitsbereiche/paedagogische-psychologie-und-motivation/personen/oettingen-gabriele/dokumente/oettingen-pak-schnetter-2001.pdf)) but the finding above is from background knowledge.

## 7. Goal progress monitoring (Harkin et al. 2016) and what it means for a weekly review and accountability

### Takeaway
Prompting people to monitor their progress raises goal attainment (d = 0.40, 138 randomized studies, N = 19,951), and the effect is larger when progress is physically recorded and when it is reported to others or made public (**Strong**). The popular "accountability partner" statistics come mostly from one non-peer-reviewed study (**Weak**). A short weekly review where he writes down progress, optionally shared with one person, is well supported.

### Cited Findings
- Harkin, Webb, Chang, Prestwich, Conner, Kellar, Benn & Sheeran (2016), "Does monitoring goal progress promote goal attainment? A meta-analysis of the experimental evidence," *Psychological Bulletin*, 142(2), 198-229: 138 studies, N = 19,951, randomized to a progress-monitoring intervention or control; interventions raised monitoring frequency (d+ = 1.98, 95% CI 1.71 to 2.24) and goal attainment (d+ = 0.40, 95% CI 0.32 to 0.48); changes in monitoring frequency mediated the effect on attainment; effects on attainment were larger when outcomes were reported to others or made public and when information was physically recorded. ([White Rose repository](https://eprints.whiterose.ac.uk/91437/); [Author manuscript PDF](https://eprints.whiterose.ac.uk/id/eprint/87431/1/bul%20harkin%20raw%20final.pdf); [APA press release](https://www.apa.org/news/press/releases/2015/10/progress-goals))
- Gail Matthews (Dominican University): 267 participants recruited from businesses and networking groups, 149 completed; those who sent weekly progress reports to a friend accomplished significantly more. The summary also notes that the often-cited "Yale/Harvard goals study" is an urban myth. No peer-reviewed publication of this study was found; the frequently quoted "76%" figure was not found in the summary. ([Dominican research summary PDF](https://www.dominican.edu/sites/default/files/2020-02/gailmatthews-harvard-goals-researchsummary.pdf))

### Inferences
- Weekly accountability is supported in the specific form Harkin describes: (1) look at progress on a schedule, (2) write it down, (3) optionally tell someone. The app's records (starts, done tasks, the 10-message count) make step 2 nearly automatic; the review screen should show them and ask one question.
- "Public" does not have to mean social media. Reporting to one chosen person (his fiancee, a classmate, a career coach) is the low-cost version. He said he may share the app with his fiancee later, so a weekly "send my week" summary is a natural fit; this is an inference, not a tested design.
- His term "positive incentive" for being intentional is consistent with the voice rules: monitoring should be framed as information, not grading. Harkin's meta-analysis does not test framing, so the no-shame framing rests on his ADHD and shame pattern, not on this evidence.
- Monitoring is the step most likely to bring shame for someone with ADHD after a missed week. The app's existing "calm return" rules (no counts after days away) should also govern the weekly review.

### Gaps
- Whether Harkin et al. found differences between monitoring behaviors versus monitoring outcomes was not extracted.
- No ADHD-specific progress-monitoring trials were found.
- Evidence on how often to review (weekly versus daily) was not found.
- Masicampo & Baumeister (2011, *JPSP*), on plan-making reducing intrusive thoughts about unfinished goals, was not retrieved (recalled only).

## 8. How many goals at once: multiple goals, goal conflict, goal shielding

### Takeaway
No study gives a "right number" of weekly goals. The available evidence points one way: a committed focal goal shields itself from competing goals, and planning tools that help with one goal can stop helping when there are six (**Moderate** for the mechanisms, **Weak** for any specific number). Fewer active goals is the safer design, especially with ADHD.

### Cited Findings
- Shah, Friedman & Kruglanski (2002), "Forgetting all else: On the antecedents and consequences of goal shielding," *Journal of Personality and Social Psychology*, 83(6), 1261-1280: activating a focal goal one is committed to inhibits the accessibility of alternative goals, more so for goals that serve the same overarching purpose; goal shielding helps goal pursuit and attainment. ([Duke Scholars record](https://scholars.duke.edu/publication/657759))
- Dalton & Spiller (2012), *Journal of Consumer Research*, 39(3), 600-614: implementation intentions helped with one goal but not with six; planning a set of goals makes their combined obstacles salient. ([IDEAS record](https://ideas.repec.org/a/oup/jconrs/doi10.1086-664500.html); [ScienceDaily](https://sciencedaily.com/releases/2012/05/120516152448.htm))
- Goal conflict and well-being: a meta-analysis exists (Gray, Ozer & Rosenthal, 2017, "Goal conflict and psychological well-being: A meta-analysis"), but its effect sizes were not retrieved. ([Bielefeld record citing it](https://pub.uni-bielefeld.de/record/2915052))
- A 2017 review of intraindividual goal conflict describes the conflict and well-being relationship as unclear, with large divergence across studies, partly due to methods and measures. ([Frontiers in Psychology review](https://www.frontiersin.org/articles/10.3389/fpsyg.2017.02011/full))

### Inferences
- The app allows up to 10 goals. The evidence suggests separating "goals I hold" (up to 10, in Life Planning) from "goals active this quarter" (2 to 3) and "this week's goals" (1 to 3, each with an if-then plan). The numbers are a design judgment, not a research finding.
- His mid-term list has six semi-goals (pass courses, connect with professors, connect with sector people, build a project, list of 20 connections, admin paperwork). Several serve the same purpose (the job), which by Shah et al. could compete with one another for attention. Grouping them under the job goal and activating one or two per week would reduce that.
- "Finish admin paperwork" is a "Keep life running" item, not a semi-goal of the job search; the app already has that goal bucket.

### Gaps
- No experimental evidence on an optimal number of weekly goals was found.
- Effect sizes of Gray et al. (2017) were not retrieved.
- Research on multiple-goal self-regulation (for example Neal, Ballard & Vancouver 2017, *Annual Review*) was not searched.
- No ADHD-specific evidence on multiple-goal pursuit was found.

## 9. Eisenhower matrix and urgency: the mere urgency effect, and does the matrix itself help?

### Takeaway
People reliably pick urgent but unimportant tasks over important ones with better payoffs, even when the urgency is fake, and more so when they feel busy; reminding people of the payoffs at the moment of choice reduced this (**Moderate**: five experiments in one paper). No experimental evidence for the Eisenhower matrix itself was found (**None found**); broader time-management behavior correlates modestly with performance and more with well-being (**Moderate**, correlational meta-analysis). His proposed order (urgent first, then urgent plus important, then important only) would build the urgency bias into the app; the standard order and the app's current code put important plus urgent first, then important.

### Cited Findings
- Zhu, Yang & Hsee (2018), "The mere urgency effect," *Journal of Consumer Research*, 45(3), 673-690: five experiments; people were more likely to do unimportant tasks with objectively lower payoffs over important tasks with better payoffs when the unimportant ones had only spurious urgency (an illusion of expiration); this violates dominance; the effect was larger in people who see themselves as busy; reminding participants of task payoffs at the moment of choice reduced the effect. ([Oxford Academic](https://academic.oup.com/jcr/advance-article/doi/10.1093/jcr/ucy030/4962206); [Johns Hopkins Hub](https://hub.jhu.edu/2018/05/31/meeting-deadlines-time-management-behaviors))
- Aeon, Faber & Panaccio (2021), "Does time management work? A meta-analysis," *PLOS ONE* (DOI 10.1371/journal.pone.0245066): 158 studies over four decades, more than 53,000 respondents; time management is moderately related to job performance, academic achievement and well-being, and negatively to distress; the strongest link is with well-being, especially life satisfaction; no discernible relation to standardized test results; conscientiousness was the main individual-difference correlate. Mostly correlational. ([EurekAlert](https://www.eurekalert.org/news-releases/878307); [BPS Research Digest](https://bps.org.uk/research-digest/good-time-management-seems-have-bigger-impact-wellbeing-work-performance); [IDEAS record](https://ideas.repec.org/a/plo/pone00/0245066.html))

### Inferences
- Step 5 of his process lists "urgent, urgent + important, important only, the rest last". If "urgent" alone comes first, the app will push email-driven, deadline-flagged items ahead of the job search, which is the textbook important-not-urgent work. The app's existing rule (`R.levelFields`: both, then important, then urgent, then nice) is the evidence-consistent order and should be kept; his written description should be corrected to match.
- Zhu et al.'s fix (show the payoff at the moment of choice) supports showing the goal's "why" next to an important task when it competes with an urgent one.
- The AI agent that reads email and calendar will mostly surface urgent items. That is useful for not missing deadlines but works against importance unless email items are attached to goals and ranked below important goal work.
- Important-not-urgent work can be given honest near-term deadlines through weekly goals ("10 messages by Friday"), which turns the urgency bias into an ally; this is an inference that matches the press advice drawn from the paper (breaking large projects into pieces with several deadlines).

### Gaps
- No study testing the Eisenhower matrix (or any urgent/important sorting tool) against a control was found.
- No replication of the mere urgency effect by other labs was located.
- No ADHD-specific evidence on urgency bias was found.

## 10. Life balance scorecards, wheel of life, and life balance research

### Takeaway
There is research on *balance* as a concept: people whose three basic needs (autonomy, competence, relatedness) are evenly satisfied report more well-being than people with the same total but uneven satisfaction (**Weak to Moderate**: four studies in the original, later replications found the balance effect small or negligible). No validation research was found for the "wheel of life" or for personal "balanced scorecards" (**None found**). A scorecard of many life areas risks adding goals and low-score shame.

### Cited Findings
- Sheldon & Niemiec (2006), "It's not just the amount that counts: Balanced need satisfaction also affects well-being," *Journal of Personality and Social Psychology*, 91, 331-341: four studies; people with balanced satisfaction of autonomy, competence and relatedness reported higher well-being than people with the same total but more variability across needs. ([World Database of Happiness record](https://worlddatabaseofhappiness.eur.nl/publications/its-not-just-the-amount-that-counts-balanced-need-satisfaction-also-affects-well-being-8275); [Typeset PDF](https://typeset.io/pdf/it-s-not-just-the-amount-that-counts-balanced-need-1gwb2l0pfp.pdf))
- Replications: in physical activity, balanced need fulfillment added "at best, negligibly" to well-being beyond individual needs; in volleyball players it added "albeit minimal" prediction. ([Journal of Exercise, Movement and Sport article](https://scapps.org/jems/index.php/1/article/view/272); [Brock University thesis](https://brocku.scholaris.ca/server/api/core/bitstreams/524d7e85-f3a4-4c01-acaa-18b80ff45d7a/content))
- The balance versus additive question is still debated within self-determination theory (2025 paper on employee well-being). ([MMU repository PDF](https://e-space.mmu.ac.uk/641617/1/1-s2.0-S0001879125000855-main.pdf))
- Time management's strongest correlate is life satisfaction (Aeon et al. 2021), which suggests the benefit of planning shows up in how life feels, not only output. ([BPS Research Digest](https://bps.org.uk/research-digest/good-time-management-seems-have-bigger-impact-wellbeing-work-performance))

### Inferences
- The "life balance scorecard" (step 3) has the weakest evidence of any step in his process. If kept, the evidence-consistent version is small: a quarterly reflection on a few areas, possibly framed through the three needs (Am I choosing my own path? Am I getting better at something? Am I connected to people?), with no scores to fall behind on.
- A scorecard "with all related goals" contradicts the goal-shielding and number-of-goals evidence (section 8). It should not generate active goals by itself; at most it flags an area to consider at the next quarterly plan.
- His triangle (the Life plan picture he plans to present) could serve the same purpose as a balance check, which would avoid a second framework.

### Gaps
- No validation study of the wheel of life coaching tool was found (the query returned nothing on it).
- Matuska and Christiansen's lifestyle balance model (2008) and the Life Balance Inventory were not retrieved in this session.
- No study of a personal balanced scorecard was found.
- No ADHD-specific life balance evidence was found.

## 11. Planning horizons (90-day versus annual) and the planning fallacy

### Takeaway
No peer-reviewed test of 90-day or quarterly planning cycles (including the "12 Week Year" book method) was found (**None found**). Indirect support: new time periods (a new week, month, semester) act as "fresh start" landmarks that raise goal starts (**Moderate**: archival field data plus one causal follow-up). The planning fallacy is robust: students predicted 34 days for a thesis and took 55 (**Strong** as a phenomenon). Corrections with some support: thinking about past similar projects, unpacking tasks into parts, and backward planning (**Weak to Moderate**, mixed).

### Cited Findings
- Buehler, Griffin & Ross (1994), "Exploring the 'planning fallacy': Why people underestimate their task completion times," *Journal of Personality and Social Psychology*, 67(3), 366-381: 37 psychology students predicted 33.9 days to finish their senior thesis on average; actual average 55.5 days; about 30% finished within their predicted time; even the average worst-case forecast (48.6 days) was too short. ([Full text PDF](https://bear.warrington.ufl.edu/brenner/mar7588/Papers/buehler-et-al-1994.pdf); [Wikipedia summary](https://www.wikipedia.org/wiki/Planning_fallacy); [SPSP blog by Buehler](https://spsp.org/news-center/blog/buehler-planning-fallacy))
- Reference class forecasting, the main structural correction, predicts an outcome from actual outcomes of a class of similar past actions; people tend to ignore their own track record and believe this project will be different. ([Wikipedia summary](https://www.wikipedia.org/wiki/Planning_fallacy); [Cambridge chapter "Inside the planning fallacy"](https://www.cambridge.org/core/books/abs/heuristics-and-biases/inside-the-planning-fallacy-the-causes-and-consequences-of-optimistic-time-predictions/CA157EF59A218C2B906A1795089E7650))
- Unpacking (Kruger & Evans 2004, *Journal of Experimental Social Psychology*): breaking a task into components before predicting reduced bias; it helps less when there are few components, components are easy, or the task is far off; some similar strategies have failed in other studies (secondary). ([Wiese, Buehler & Griffin 2016, which reviews it](https://dlab.sauder.ubc.ca/sjdm/journal/16/16101/jdm16101.html))
- Wiese, Buehler & Griffin (2016), "Backward planning: Effects of planning direction on predictions of task completion time," *Judgment and Decision Making*, 11(2), 147-167: backward planners predicted longer completion times and, in a follow-up of real projects, were less biased than controls; backward planning seemed to increase attention to things that delay progress; authors call the support partial. ([Journal full text](https://dlab.sauder.ubc.ca/sjdm/journal/16/16101/jdm16101.html); [IDEAS record](https://ideas.repec.org/a/cup/judgdm/v11y2016i2p147-167_2.html))
- Fresh start effect: Dai, Milkman & Riis (2014), *Management Science*: Google searches for "diet", gym visits, and commitments to pursue goals all rise after temporal landmarks (start of a week, month, year, semester; birthdays; holidays). A 2015 *Psychological Science* follow-up gave causal evidence that emphasizing a landmark that marks a new period increases intentions to start goals. The authors note limited insight into mechanisms. ([Full text PDF](https://anderson-review.ucla.edu/wp-content/uploads/2021/03/Dai-Milkman-Riis_FreshStartEffect_2014_Mgmt_Sci.pdf); [Penn CHIBE publication page](https://chibe.upenn.edu/publications/the-fresh-start-effect-breaking-points-in-life-motivate-virtuous-behavior/); [Wharton Knowledge](https://knowledge.wharton.upenn.edu/article/need-fresh-start-heres-begin/))
- The search found no research on the "12 Week Year" method or on quarterly versus annual goal cycles. ([Search result context: fresh start sources above](https://chibe.upenn.edu/news/the-fresh-start-effect/))

### Inferences
- A 90-day plan is a reasonable *design choice* but should be described as such, not as evidence-based. Its best arguments are indirect: it is closer than a year (helpful given ADHD's steeper delay discounting, section 2), it gives four fresh-start moments a year, and a semester at Berkeley is already about that long (aligning the 90-day plan with the semester would make it a natural landmark).
- Weekly planning on Monday (or Sunday evening) uses the week as a fresh-start landmark; a missed week then has a built-in restart, which fits the calm return rules.
- For time estimates, the app could use his own past durations (the open idea "minutes from history") as the reference class. As a rough rule of thumb from Buehler's data, actual time was about 1.6 times the prediction (55.5 / 33.9); this ratio is from one small student sample and is only an illustration.
- For the job goal ("start within a year"), the planning fallacy implies his milestone dates are likely too optimistic; the backward plan (section 12) should leave slack.

### Gaps
- No study comparing 90-day and annual goal horizons was found.
- No peer-reviewed evaluation of the "12 Week Year" was found.
- No research on the planning fallacy or time-estimation in adults with ADHD was searched in this session (time perception in ADHD may be covered by other researchers).

## 12. Backward planning from the goal (Park, Lu & Hedgcock 2017)

### Takeaway
Planning backward from the goal date to today led to more motivation, higher expectancy of success, less time pressure and better performance than planning forward, but only for complex goals; for simple goals it made no difference (**Moderate to Weak**: one paper with five studies of about 300 university students; follow-up theses mixed). His job goal is complex, so backward planning fits it.

### Cited Findings
- Park, Lu & Hedgcock (2017), "Relative effects of forward and backward planning on goal pursuit," *Psychological Science*, 28(11), 1620-1630 (DOI 10.1177/0956797617715510): relative to forward planning, backward planning produced greater motivation, higher goal expectancy, less time pressure and better goal-relevant performance; the benefit held only for complex goals (for example a comprehensive final exam) and disappeared for simple goals; authors suggest starting from the end made intermediate steps clearer. ([APS news](https://psychologicalscience.org/news/minds-business/trying-to-get-ahead-plan-in-reverse-study-suggests.html); [APS journal page](https://www.psychologicalscience.org/journals/psychological-science/0956797617715510/); [Peking University HSBC Business School page](https://english.phbs.pku.edu.cn/info/3521/23601.htm); [University of Minnesota record](https://experts.umn.edu/en/publications/relative-effects-of-forward-and-backward-planning-on-goal-pursuit/))
- Five studies with about 300 university students (secondary). ([KQED MindShift](https://www.kqed.org/mindshift/50947/how-reverse-planning-for-goals-can-help-students-succeed-in-school))
- A Vienna thesis found backward planning did not significantly improve plan clarity or reduce planning difficulty compared with forward planning. ([University of Vienna thesis](https://utheses-gateway.univie.ac.at/api/document/get/file/72225))
- Wiese, Buehler & Griffin (2016): backward planning lengthened and in one real-project study de-biased time predictions (see section 11). ([Journal full text](https://dlab.sauder.ubc.ca/sjdm/journal/16/16101/jdm16101.html))

### Inferences
- His semi-goals are already the middle of a backward plan. Making it explicit: start from "job starts by [month, year]", then offer by about 2 to 3 months before, interviews before that, then conversations, then the list of 20 connections and the conversation-starter project, then this week's 10 messages. Each step gets a date, and the weekly goal is read off the plan.
- Because benefits held only for complex goals, there is no reason to backward-plan simple items (homework, paperwork).
- With the planning fallacy, the backward plan should put the earliest steps (outreach, project) as early as possible and leave buffer near the end.

### Gaps
- Effect sizes for Park et al. were not extracted.
- A 2019 dissertation (Boulton) reported in a search summary as finding backward planning more motivating for short-term but not long-term goals could not be tied to a confirmed source URL.
- No ADHD-specific backward planning evidence was found.

## 13. Life-crafting and goal-setting writing interventions for students (Schippers et al. 2015; Morisano et al. 2010)

### Takeaway
Early studies reported large gains from a few hours of online writing about personal goals and plans (GPA 2.91 versus 2.46 in struggling students; achievement gaps closing by 38% to 98%), but those were a small RCT (n = 85) and a quasi-experimental cohort comparison. A large, preregistered-scale RCT with about 1,400 students found no effect on grades, credits or persistence (**Mixed / Weak**). A one-time "life plan" writing exercise is cheap and may help with direction, but should not be expected to drive results by itself.

### Cited Findings
- Morisano, Hirsh, Peterson, Pihl & Shore (2010), "Setting, elaborating, and reflecting on personal goals improves academic performance," *Journal of Applied Psychology*, 95(2), 255-264: 85 struggling undergraduates; online goal-setting program; next-semester GPA 2.91 versus 2.46 for the comparison group; more students kept a full course load; the What Works Clearinghouse rates it as meeting standards without reservations. ([WWC review](https://ies.ed.gov/ncee/WWC/Study/81535); [Full text PDF](https://archive.otis.edu/sites/default/files/GoalSettingJAP2010.pdf))
- Dobronyi, Oreopoulos & Petronijevic (2019), "Goal setting, academic reminders, and college success: A large-scale field experiment," *Journal of Research on Educational Effectiveness*: about 1,400 first-year students at a large Canadian university randomized to one of two online goal-setting treatments or control; no evidence of effects on GPA, course credits or second-year persistence; precise enough to detect a 7% of a standard deviation effect, versus about 70% of a standard deviation in Morisano et al.; the authors could not reproduce the original's self-nominated struggling subgroup exactly. ([NBER working paper](https://nber.org/papers/w23738); [Author PDF](https://oreopoulos.faculty.economics.utoronto.ca/wp-content/uploads/2020/05/dobronyi-et-al-goal-setting-academic-reminders-and-college-success-jree-2019.pdf); [Journal article](https://informahealthcare.com/doi/full/10.1080/19345747.2018.1517849))
- Schippers, Scheepers & Peterson (2015), "A scalable goal-setting intervention closes both the gender and ethnic minority achievement gap," *Palgrave Communications*, 1, 15014: N = 703 first-year students at a large European business school (Rotterdam School of Management); the gender gap closed by 98% after year one; the ethnicity gap closed by 38% after year one and 93% after year two; higher achievement and retention, especially for ethnic minority and male students. Quasi-experimental (cohort comparison). ([Nature / Palgrave article](https://www.nature.com/articles/palcomms201514); [Erasmus summary](https://www.eur.nl/en/testimonials/investigation-how-evidence-based-goal-setting-intervention-closed-gender-and-ethnic-achievement-gap))
- Schippers et al. (2020), "Writing about personal goals and plans regardless of goal type boosts academic performance," *Contemporary Educational Psychology*, 60. ([Erasmus repository PDF](https://repub.eur.nl/pub/123006/Schippers-et.al-2020-CEP.pdf))
- A later review notes the null RCT contrasts with Morisano and Schippers and calls for more conclusive evidence, citing small samples and quasi-experimental designs in earlier work. ([CORE review PDF](https://core.ac.uk/download/401696119.pdf))

### Inferences
- A one-time life-crafting exercise (writing values, ideal future, goals and plans) is a reasonable entry point to "Life Planning", and it naturally produces the "why" for each goal. The strongest evidence in these notes (implementation intentions, monitoring) is about what happens *after* the writing, so the app's value lies in the weekly loop, not the initial essay.
- Life-crafting includes positive future writing; by section 6 it should end with obstacles and plans, which the Schippers protocol does include in its later steps (based on the general description of the intervention as goal-directed conceptualization and action; protocol details not extracted here).

### Gaps
- The exact protocol steps of the Schippers life-crafting intervention and its effect sizes in the 2020 paper were not extracted.
- No life-crafting study in students with ADHD was found.

## 14. ADHD-specific evidence (collected in one place)

### Takeaway
ADHD-specific evidence for these techniques exists only for if-then plans and MCII, and only in children, mostly in lab tasks or short-term parent ratings, mostly from one research group (**Weak to Moderate**). The best-established ADHD fact relevant to goal hierarchies is steeper delay discounting (d = .43, N = 3,913), which argues for short horizons and proximal goals. No trials in adults with ADHD of goal setting, MCII, progress monitoring, backward planning, or life-crafting were found in this session.

### Cited Findings
- Delay discounting: d = 0.43 across 25 case-control comparisons (N = 3,913); no publication bias detected (Jackson & MacKillop 2016). ([PubMed](https://www.pubmed.gov/27722208/); [PMC](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC5049699))
- Adults with ADHD: d = 0.40 (secondary). ([PMC review](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC7912943/))
- If-then plans brought Go/NoGo inhibition in children with ADHD to the level of controls; with stimulant medication the combination was best (Gawrilow & Gollwitzer 2008). ([Konstanz page](https://www.socmot.uni-konstanz.de/publications/implementation-intentions-facilitate-response-inhibition-adhd-children))
- If-then plans and methylphenidate each normalized Go/NoGo inhibition in children with ADHD (Paul-Jordanov, Bechtold & Gawrilow 2010). ([Springer](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s12402-010-0028-9))
- If-then plans and delay of gratification in children with and without ADHD (Gawrilow, Gollwitzer & Oettingen 2011; results not extracted). ([PDF](https://www.socmot.uni-konstanz.de/sites/default/files/Gawrilow_Gollwitzer_Oettingen_Delay_2011-2.pdf))
- MCII improved parent-rated self-regulation in sixth and seventh graders, most in those at risk for ADHD (Gawrilow et al. 2013, *Motivation and Emotion*, not a psychiatry journal). ([Springer](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s11031-012-9288-3))
- Participation in goal setting raised self-efficacy and skill in learning-disabled children (Schunk; secondary). ([Typeset record](https://typeset.io/pdf/participation-in-goal-setting-effects-on-self-efficacy-and-3r9necwxog.pdf))

### Inferences
- For an adult graduate student with ADHD, the transfer from these child lab studies is plausible but untested. The design implications that rest on broad evidence (if-then plans, monitoring, proximal goals, learning goals) do not depend on the ADHD studies being right; the ADHD studies only suggest these tools are not blocked by ADHD and may help more.
- Steeper discounting is the main reason the weekly and daily levels matter more for him than the years level.

### Gaps
- Adult ADHD interventions that include planning and goal components (CBT for adult ADHD, meta-cognitive therapy, ADHD coaching) were outside this search and may be covered by other researchers.
- Paul, Gawrilow et al. (2007) was not verified.
- No study of planning fallacy, construal level or urgency bias in ADHD was found.

## 15. Overall: what the evidence suggests he should change in the proposed structure

### Takeaway
The skeleton (years, then months, then weeks, then days, with weekly review) is well supported. The evidence suggests six changes: fix the urgency order, make weekly goals behaviors and learning goals rather than outcomes, add an obstacle-and-plan step to every "aspirational" goal, keep few goals active, keep AI-suggested goals under his choice, and downgrade the life balance scorecard to an optional reflection.

### Cited Findings
- Urgent-first sorting matches the mere urgency effect; reminding of payoffs reduced it. ([Zhu, Yang & Hsee 2018](https://academic.oup.com/jcr/advance-article/doi/10.1093/jcr/ucy030/4962206))
- Learning goals beat outcome goals on new complex tasks. ([Winters & Latham 1996](https://bvbvf1.bib-bvb.de/vufind/PrimoRecord/cdi_proquest_journals_203376963); [Seijts & Latham 2001](https://zendy.io/title/10.1002/job.70))
- Positive fantasies about a job predicted fewer applications and offers; MCII improves attainment. ([Oettingen & Mayer 2002](https://www.psy.uni-hamburg.de/arbeitsbereiche/paedagogische-psychologie-und-motivation/personen/oettingen-gabriele/dokumente/oettingen-mayer-2002.pdf); [Wang et al. 2021](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC8149892))
- Planning helps one goal but not six; focal goals shield themselves. ([Dalton & Spiller 2012](https://ideas.repec.org/a/oup/jconrs/doi10.1086-664500.html); [Shah et al. 2002](https://scholars.duke.edu/publication/657759))
- Autonomous goals predict progress, controlled goals do not. ([Koestner et al. 2008](https://vufind.lboro.ac.uk/PrimoRecord/cdi_proquest_miscellaneous_69592016); [Koestner et al. 2002](https://www.selfdeterminationtheory.org/SDT/documents/2002_KoestnerLekesPowersChicoine_JPSP.pdf))
- Monitoring, especially recorded and shared, raises attainment. ([Harkin et al. 2016](https://eprints.whiterose.ac.uk/91437/))
- Backward planning helps complex goals. ([Park, Lu & Hedgcock 2017](https://psychologicalscience.org/news/minds-business/trying-to-get-ahead-plan-in-reverse-study-suggests.html))

### Inferences
Concrete changes, most supported first:
1. **Sorting order (step 5).** Use important plus urgent, then important only, then urgent only, then the rest. This is what the app's code already does; his written process says urgent first and should be corrected. Show the goal's why next to important tasks at choice moments. (Strong reasoning from Zhu et al.; matrix itself untested.)
2. **Weekly goals (step 4).** Write them as behaviors he controls ("10 outreach messages", "homework in by the deadline") plus at most one learning goal ("find out what 3 climate adaptation funds look for in analysts"). Avoid weekly outcome goals that others control (replies, interviews, offers). (Moderate.)
3. **If-then plan per weekly goal.** One plan, in his words, tied to a real cue in his week, read back at the review. (Strong.)
4. **"Aspirational but achievable" becomes WOOP.** For each long-term and 90-day goal: wish, best outcome, inner obstacle, if-then plan, and a quick "how likely?" check; reshape goals he rates unlikely instead of pushing harder. Avoid features that only make him picture success. (Moderate; one ADHD child study.)
5. **Few active goals.** Up to 10 held goals, 2 to 3 active per quarter, 1 to 3 per week. Move "finish admin paperwork" to "Keep life running". (Moderate mechanism, numbers are a judgment.)
6. **AI suggestions stay suggestions (step 2c).** The AI maps email and calendar items onto his existing goals as tasks; any new goal it proposes needs his acceptance and his own why. (Moderate.)
7. **Backward plan the job goal (mid-term).** From the start date back to this week, with dates on each semi-goal and slack for the planning fallacy. (Moderate to weak.)
8. **Weekly review (step 6).** Short, written, shown from the app's own records, optionally sent to one person; same no-shame rules as the calm return. (Strong for monitoring; framing is a design choice.)
9. **Time estimates.** Use his past durations as the reference class; expect plans to run long. (Strong phenomenon; corrections mixed.)
10. **90-day horizon.** Fine as a design choice aligned with the semester and fresh-start landmarks; do not present it as research-backed. (None found for 90 days specifically.)
11. **Life balance scorecard (step 3).** Make it optional, qualitative, quarterly, and unable to create active goals by itself; or fold it into his triangle picture. (Weak.)
12. **Show why at choice, first step at start.** Keep the existing why per goal and first small step per task, and place each on the screen where it does its job. (Moderate.)

### Gaps
- No research was found on "positive incentive" framing of accountability, or on rewards for weekly goal completion in adults with ADHD.
- No trial was found of a full multi-level goal system (years to days) of the kind he proposes, with or without ADHD; the recommendations combine evidence about the separate parts.
