Method note for the report writer: all findings below come from web search results (abstracts, publisher pages, PubMed records, guideline pages, press releases). Full-text fetches failed because two hosts are blocked in this session's network settings: pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov and files.eric.ed.gov. Numbers marked "per summary" were not checked against the full paper. Nothing here is invented; where a number could not be found it is listed under Gaps.

## 1. What is the current scientific account of task initiation problems in ADHD? (Barkley, Sonuga-Barke, dopamine and reward)

### Takeaway
The leading accounts agree that ADHD is not a problem of knowing what to do but of acting on it when the payoff is far away: Barkley frames it as weak self-regulation across time (a deficit in "doing what you know" at the point of performance), Sonuga-Barke adds a separate motivational pathway (delay aversion), and PET studies link lower dopamine reward-pathway markers in adults to inattention and lower trait motivation. Evidence strength: the theories are well established and influential, but the dopamine findings are correlational, from one lab, with samples under 100.

### Cited Findings
**Barkley's self-regulation / executive function model**
- Barkley (1997), "Behavioral inhibition, sustained attention, and executive functions: constructing a unifying theory of ADHD" / "ADHD, self-regulation, and time: toward a more comprehensive theory," Psychological Bulletin 121(1):65-94. Places behavioral inhibition at the center and ties it to four executive functions: nonverbal working memory, self-regulation of affect/motivation/arousal, internalization of speech, and reconstitution. The model predicts impairments in regulation of motivation, not only attention. [PubMed 9276836](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/9276836/); [Full text PDF of Barkley 1997](https://sciences.ucf.edu/psychology/childrenslearningclinic/wp-content/uploads/sites/24/2013/08/Barkley-1997-Psych-Bulletin.pdf)
- Barkley defines self-regulation as self-directed action meant to change future behavior "so as to alter the probability of a delayed consequence," and describes impulsive motivation as a preference for immediate gratification with greater discounting of delayed consequences. (per summary) [Barkley, ADHD Self-Regulation and Executive Functioning handout](https://cba-va.org/wp-content/uploads/2023/04/ADHD-Self-Regulation-and-Executive-Functioning-Russell-A.-Barkley-Ph.D..pdf)
- "Point of performance": Barkley's treatment principle that effective help must happen in the actual place and moment where the behavior is required, because people with ADHD have trouble bringing the imagined future to bear on the present; continuing-education material summarizes it as "all effective treatments must be at the point of performance." (secondary summaries) [Continuing education course outline](https://continuingedcourses.net/course/adhd-in-children-diagnosis-assessment-and-management/); [GreatSchools summary of Barkley's theory](https://www.greatschools.org/gk/parenting/adhd/dr-russell-barkley-ad-hd-theory-diagnosis-and-treatment-summary/)
- Barkley's companion clinical principles are widely summarized as "externalize" information, time and motivation (put reminders, clocks and rewards in the physical environment). I could not find a source in this session that quotes the exact wording "externalize motivation," so treat this phrasing as unverified here; check Barkley's books (Executive Functions, 2012; Taking Charge of Adult ADHD). [Guilford excerpt, Barkley 2018 chapter 9](https://www.guilford.com/excerpts/barkley18_ch9.pdf?t=1)

**Sonuga-Barke's dual pathway model and delay aversion**
- The dual pathway model (Sonuga-Barke 2002, 2003) proposes two partly separate routes to ADHD: an executive pathway (inhibition, working memory deficits) and a motivational pathway, delay aversion, in which the person tries to escape or avoid delay; delay aversion is linked to an altered delay-of-reward gradient in the mesolimbic dopamine circuit (nucleus accumbens). [University of Southampton eprints 66171](https://eprints.soton.ac.uk/66171); [University of Southampton eprints 18285](https://eprints.soton.ac.uk/18285/)
- A later review notes delay aversion characterizes some, but not all, patients with ADHD (heterogeneity is part of the model). [Neuropsychology Review 2009](https://link.springer.com/doi/10.1007/s11065-009-9115-0)
- Tobias et al. (2009, Neuropsychology): choice-delay task in 360 ADHD probands, 349 siblings, 112 controls; concluded that choosing small-sooner rewards may mark an ADHD motivational subtype. (per summary; this is mostly a child/adolescent sample) [PMC2788754 via search](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC2788754)

**Dopamine and reward findings in adults**
- Volkow et al. (2009), "Evaluating dopamine reward pathway in ADHD: clinical implications," JAMA 302(10):1084-1091. PET in 53 never-medicated adults with ADHD vs 44 controls (Brookhaven, 2001-2009). Dopamine transporter and D2/D3 receptor availability were lower in ADHD in reward-pathway regions (for example nucleus accumbens DAT 0.71 controls vs 0.63 ADHD; midbrain 0.16 vs 0.09) and D2/D3 levels correlated with inattention ratings. [JAMA abstract](https://jamanetwork.com/journals/jama/article-abstract/184547); [Brookhaven press release](https://www.bnl.gov/newsroom/news.php?a=110998)
- Volkow et al. (2011), "Motivation deficit in ADHD is associated with dysfunction of the dopamine reward pathway," Molecular Psychiatry 16(11):1147-1154 (online Sept 2010, doi 10.1038/mp.2010.97). 45 adults with ADHD vs 41 controls; lower D2/D3 and DAT in nucleus accumbens and midbrain correlated with lower scores on the Achievement scale of the Multidimensional Personality Questionnaire (trait motivation) in the ADHD group (P<0.02) but not in controls. [Nature](https://www.nature.com/articles/mp201097); [PubMed 20856250](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/20856250/)
- Plichta & Scheres (2014), Neuroscience & Biobehavioral Reviews 38:125-134, meta-analysis of fMRI studies: ventral striatum is hyporesponsive during reward anticipation in ADHD, Cohen's d about 0.48 to 0.58 (medium). Some studies were coded as zero effect because they reported no significant striatal finding, and later studies have failed to replicate it in some samples. (per summary) [PMC3989497](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3989497); [Heidelberg record](https://heibib.ub.uni-heidelberg.de/search/Record/1548762857)
- One study found reduced ventral-striatal reward anticipation in adults with ADHD but not in children with combined type, leaving open whether the hypoactivity develops over time. (per summary) [Social Cognitive and Affective Neuroscience 10(7):945](https://academic.oup.com/scan/article/10/7/945/1735806)

### Inferences
- All three accounts point at the same practical gap Marcelo's design targets: the person knows the goal, but the goal does not reliably produce action now. Barkley's view implies that knowing WHY is necessary but not sufficient; the WHY must be made present at the moment of starting (visible, concrete, tied to the specific first step), which is what "point of performance" means in practice.
- The Volkow 2011 result (motivation tied to reward-pathway markers) supports treating low motivation as part of ADHD rather than a character flaw, which fits the app's no-shame voice rules.
- Evidence strength: Barkley and Sonuga-Barke models are strong as frameworks (heavily cited, partly supported by task data) but neither is proven as a full causal account. Dopamine imaging evidence is moderate at best: medium effects, small samples, cross-sectional, mostly one lab for the PET work.

### Gaps
- Sonuga-Barke, Bitsakou & Thompson (2010) "triple pathway" model (adding temporal processing deficits) did not appear in search results; it should be checked directly (J Am Acad Child Adolesc Psychiatry 2010).
- Time perception / "time blindness" meta-analyses in adults were not searched; Barkley's time-blindness claim should not be cited with numbers from these notes.
- No independent replication of the Volkow PET findings was found in this session.

## 2. Delay discounting in ADHD: how much steeper, and can a goal 1 to 3 years away motivate starting a task today?

### Takeaway
People with ADHD discount delayed money rewards more steeply than controls, with a medium effect (d about 0.43 to 0.47) that meta-analyses find does not shrink with age; this is a shift in the average, with heavy overlap between groups, not an inability to value the future. Evidence strength: strong and replicated for the existence and size of the effect on lab money tasks; weak for what it means for real life goals years away, because no study tests that directly.

### Cited Findings
- Jackson & MacKillop (2016), "Attention-Deficit/Hyperactivity Disorder and Monetary Delay Discounting: A Meta-Analysis of Case-Control Studies," Biological Psychiatry: Cognitive Neuroscience and Neuroimaging 1:316-325. 21 studies, 25 case-control comparisons, N = 3913. Steeper discounting in ADHD, d = 0.43 (p < 10^-15). No significant moderation by sample age, reward type (real vs hypothetical) or comorbidity. Excluding medicated samples gave d = 0.45 (as reported by a later paper). [ScienceDirect](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S2451902216000914); [ResearchGate](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/294422472_Attention-DeficitHyperactivity_Disorder_and_Monetary_Delay_Discounting_A_Meta-Analysis_of_Case-Control_Studies)
- Patros et al. (2016, Clinical Psychology Review), meta-analysis of choice impulsivity, d = 0.47, described by others as comparable to Jackson & MacKillop. (cited secondhand) [Child Neuropsychology 2018 article citing both](https://www.tandfonline.com/doi/full/10.1080/09297049.2018.1508563)
- Marx et al. (2021), "ADHD and the choice of small immediate over larger delayed rewards: a comparative meta-analysis of performance on simple choice-delay and temporal discounting paradigms," Journal of Attention Disorders 25(2):171-187. 37 group comparisons (22 simple choice-delay, 15 temporal discounting), 3,763 participants (53% ADHD). Small-to-medium effects for both paradigms. Using real rewards in the simple choice-delay paradigm almost doubled the odds ratio for ADHD. The authors describe ADHD choice behavior as a continuum (from less to more discounting), not a uniform steep discounting. Mixed child and adult samples. [King's College London record](https://kclpure.kcl.ac.uk/portal/en/publications/adhd-and-the-choice-of-small-immediate-over-larger-delayed-reward/); [ResearchGate](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/324770261_Attention-deficithyperactivity_disorder_ADHD_and_the_choice_of_small_immediate_over_larger_delayed_rewards_A_comparative_meta-analysis_of_performance_on_simple_choice-delay_and_temporal_discounting_pa)
- A 2024 review states that all meta-analyses report steeper delay discounting in ADHD than controls, generally with medium effect sizes, and discusses it as a possible intermediate phenotype shared with substance use disorder. [Current Addiction Reports 2024](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s40429-024-00558-y)
- Doidge, Flora & Toplak (2021, Journal of Attention Disorders) meta-analyzed sex differences on delay-of-gratification and temporal discounting tasks in ADHD and typical samples. (effect sizes not retrieved) [SAGE](https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/abs/10.1177/1087054718815588)
- Adult-specific: Mowinckel et al. (2015, Journal of Attention Disorders), "A meta-analysis of decision-making and attention in adults with ADHD," covers adult decision tasks including delay discounting. (exact effect sizes not retrieved in this session) [PubMed 25477020](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/25477020/)
- A 2021 Frontiers in Psychiatry paper is described as a systematic review/meta-analysis on ADHD and delay discounting in adults; its results were not visible in search snippets. [Frontiers in Psychiatry 2021](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/psychiatry/articles/10.3389/fpsyt.2021.519840/full)
- Counterpoint: a 2022 study concluded ADHD symptoms relate to sub-optimal and inconsistent temporal decision-making rather than simply steeper discounting. [ResearchGate, 2022](https://researchgate.net/publication/363927764_Symptoms_of_Attention_DeficitHyperactivity_Disorder_Are_Associated_with_Sub_Optimal_and_Inconsistent_Temporal_Decision_Making/fulltext/63359333ff870c55cee7ec9d/Symptoms-of-Attention-Deficit-Hyperactivity-Disorder-Are-Associated-with-Sub-Optimal-and-Inconsistent-Temporal-Decision-Making.pdf)
- Self-report work links ADHD, delay discounting and risky financial behavior in adults (preliminary). [PLOS ONE 2017](https://journals.plos.org/plosone/article?id=10.1371%2Fjournal.pone.0176933)
- Episodic future thinking (imagining a specific personal future event in vivid detail) is a studied method for reducing delay discounting in general adult samples (prediabetes, obesity, hypertension), with considerable variation in methods; no trial in an ADHD sample was found. [Review, PMC9669959](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC9669959/)

### Inferences
- A d of about 0.45 means the average person with ADHD values a delayed reward noticeably less than the average control, but the distributions overlap heavily (most people with ADHD fall within the normal range on any single task). So "distant goals cannot motivate people with ADHD" is not supported; "distant goals lose more of their pull in the moment, on average" is.
- Lab delays are usually seconds to months and the rewards are money. A goal 1 to 3 years away is far outside the tested range, so its pull at the moment of starting is likely weak for anyone, and weaker on average with ADHD. This argues for Marcelo's design to treat the long-term WHY as a source of direction and meaning (what to choose) rather than as the fuel for starting today's task.
- The design implication that follows from Barkley plus discounting: bring the distant goal forward. Show the WHY next to today's first step, break it into near-term milestones (weekly goals), and add something immediate at the moment of starting (a visible completion, a short timer ending, a check mark). Marx et al.'s finding that real (tangible) rewards double the ADHD effect also suggests the immediacy and concreteness of the payoff matter, not just its size.
- Episodic future thinking is a plausible, testable feature (picture the specific future moment the goal serves) but it has no ADHD-specific evidence yet; frame it as an experiment.
- Evidence strength: strong for a medium group difference on lab tasks; moderate that it holds in adults (age was not a moderator in Jackson & MacKillop); weak-to-none on years-long goals.

### Gaps
- No adult-only delay discounting effect size was retrieved (Mowinckel 2015 and the 2021 Frontiers review exist but their numbers were not visible).
- No study found that tests whether long-term personal goals (1 to 3 years) predict task initiation in ADHD, or whether goal-linking interventions help initiation in ADHD.
- Rung & Madden (2018) meta-analysis of episodic future thinking effects on discounting was not retrieved; effect size not available here.
- Implementation intentions ("if-then plans") and mental contrasting have child ADHD studies (Gawrilow, Gollwitzer, Oettingen) that were not retrieved in this session; adult evidence unknown.

## 3. Immediate vs delayed reward, reward vs punishment / response cost, and sensitivity to punishment or frustration

### Takeaway
Reinforcement (both reward and response cost) improves performance in children with ADHD, more than in controls, which shows that performance depends heavily on immediate consequences; emerging child studies suggest heightened sensitivity to punishment, and adults with ADHD show large emotional dysregulation (g = 1.17), which plausibly feeds avoidance after failure. Evidence strength: moderate for reinforcement effects (mostly children), weak and child-only for punishment sensitivity, strong for adult emotional dysregulation.

### Cited Findings
- Luman, Oosterlaan & Sergeant (2005), "The impact of reinforcement contingencies on AD/HD: a review and theoretical appraisal," Clinical Psychology Review 25:183-213 (doi 10.1016/j.cpr.2004.11.001). Reviewed 22 studies, N = 1181 children. Both reward and response cost improved task performance in ADHD and controls; per the corrigendum, improvement was more prominent in ADHD. Children only. [ResearchGate record](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/8088066_Luman_M_Oosterlaan_J_Sergeant_JA_The_impact_of_reinforcement_contingencies_on_ADHD_a_review_and_theoretical_appraisal_Clin_Psychol_Rev_25_183-213); [Corrigendum, ScienceDirect](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S0272735805000243)
- Luman et al. (2008), "Decision-making in ADHD: sensitive to frequency but blind to the magnitude of penalty?" Journal of Child Psychology and Psychiatry: title states the finding that children with ADHD tracked how often penalties came but not how large they were. (child sample; abstract details not retrieved) [Wiley](https://acamh.onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/10.1111/j.1469-7610.2008.01910.x)
- Oosterlaan & Sergeant (1998): reward and response cost tested in a crossover design on response inhibition in ADHD, disruptive, anxious and typical children, testing whether a motivational deficit underlies poor inhibition. [PubMed 9650623](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/9650623/?dopt=Abstract)
- Furukawa et al. (2017), "Evidence for increased behavioral control by punishment in children with ADHD" (J Child Psychol Psychiatry): punishment sensitivity has received limited attention; the two available studies on positive punishment (Furukawa 2017, 2019) show increased, not decreased, sensitivity to punishment in children with ADHD. Children only. [ResearchGate](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/307969287_Evidence_for_increased_behavioral_control_by_punishment_in_children_with_attention-deficit_hyperactivity_disorder); [BMC Psychiatry 2020 citing it](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1186/s12888-020-2442-7)
- Beheshti, Chavanon & Christiansen (2020), "Emotion dysregulation in adults with ADHD: a meta-analysis," BMC Psychiatry 20:120. Adults with ADHD showed much higher general emotion dysregulation than healthy controls, Hedges' g = 1.17 (p < 0.001); emotional lability and negative emotional responses were the most important facets. [BMC Psychiatry](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1186/s12888-020-2442-7); [PubMed 32164655](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/32164655/)
- Frustration account (from Amsel's frustration theory, applied to ADHD): children with ADHD may struggle to develop frustration tolerance because they experience stronger negative arousal when an expected reward is withheld. (theory, not established finding) [BMC Psychiatry 2020](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1186/s12888-020-2442-7)
- Response timing studies show reinforcement valence (reward vs loss) and magnitude modulate performance in ADHD. [Journal of Abnormal Child Psychology 2007](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s10802-007-9190-8)

### Inferences
- Immediate consequences shape ADHD performance more than average. For the app this supports small, immediate, positive feedback at the moment of starting and finishing, rather than relying on a distant goal alone.
- Punishment and response cost do change behavior, but combined with heightened negative emotional reactions in adults (g = 1.17) and possibly heightened punishment sensitivity, guilt-based or loss-framed designs (red "overdue", broken streaks, lost points) are likely to raise avoidance. This supports the app's existing no-shame rules. It also warns that a long-term goal shown as a gap ("you are behind on your 3-year plan") could act as a punishment cue and trigger avoidance.
- Evidence strength: the reinforcement literature is mostly child lab tasks; transfer to adult daily life is an assumption. The adult emotion dysregulation meta-analysis is strong.

### Gaps
- No adult study of punishment sensitivity was found. "Rejection sensitive dysphoria" (a popular Dodson term) was not found in peer-reviewed form in this session and should be treated as a popular construct.
- Luman, Tripp & Scheres (2010, Neuroscience & Biobehavioral Reviews) reward-processing review was not retrieved.

## 4. Is the "interest-based nervous system" (William Dodson) supported? What is known about interest, novelty, urgency and challenge?

### Takeaway
No peer-reviewed study was found that tests Dodson's "interest-based nervous system" as a construct; it is a clinician's description that a peer-reviewed social work article treats as a community narrative, and its claim to explain every aspect of ADHD goes beyond the evidence. Its ingredients are partly consistent with real findings (stronger dependence on immediate, salient reward; hyperfocus self-reports), but the specific claim that importance and distant rewards do not motivate ADHD brains is not established. Evidence strength: popular claim without direct support; indirect support is moderate for "immediate and salient rewards matter more," weak for anything more specific.

### Cited Findings
- No peer-reviewed paper testing the "interest-based nervous system" was found; one blog reports a Europe PMC search for the phrase returned no papers while "delay aversion" and ADHD returned 589 (blog claim, not verified here). [Science Works Health blog](https://www.scienceworkshealth.com/post/interest-based-nervous-system-adhd)
- A peer-reviewed article in the British Journal of Social Work (2023) discusses Dodson's model as coming from an ADHD community theorist, part of a "counter narrative" from deficit to difference, not as a tested finding. [British Journal of Social Work 53(8):3647](https://academic.oup.com/bjsw/article/53/8/3647/7181434)
- Dodson's own materials (ADDitude) present the idea with "rejection sensitive dysphoria" and "hyperfocus" as defining features; these are practitioner articles, not studies. [ADDitude](https://www.additudemag.com/symptoms-of-add-hyperarousal-rejection-sensitivity/); [Dodson PDF](https://adhd.dk/wp-content/uploads/sites/2/2022/05/Dodson-How-ADHD-Shapes-Your-Perceptions-Emotions-.pdf)
- The popular "PINCH" framing (Passion/Play, Interest, Novelty, Competition, Hurry/urgency) circulates on clinician blogs without peer-reviewed testing. [Neurodivergent Insights](https://neurodivergentinsights.com/working-with-your-interest-based-nervous-system/)
- Hyperfocus: Hupfeld, Abagis & Shah (2019), "Living 'in the zone': hyperfocus in adult ADHD," ADHD Attention Deficit and Hyperactivity Disorders 11:191-208. Pilot sample n = 251 and replication n = 372 adults; those meeting ADHD threshold scored higher on the new Adult Hyperfocus Questionnaire, with scores correlating with symptom severity. A 2024 dispositional version was validated in 347 adults. Self-report survey data. [PubMed 30267329](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/30267329/)
- Contrary finding (per search summary): a study of 78 ADHD patients vs matched controls found no difference in occurrence, frequency, duration or pervasiveness of hyperfocus, though in ADHD hyperfocus was less likely in educational and social settings; its authors concluded hyperfocus is not specific to ADHD. The specific paper could not be identified with certainty in this session. [Search result set incl. Psychological Research](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s00426-019-01245-8)
- Indirect support for "salient, immediate payoff matters more": reinforcement improves ADHD performance more than controls (Luman 2005), real rewards nearly double the ADHD effect in choice-delay tasks (Marx 2021), and reward-pathway markers correlate with motivation in adults with ADHD (Volkow 2011). [Luman 2005 corrigendum](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S0272735805000243); [Marx 2021](https://kclpure.kcl.ac.uk/portal/en/publications/adhd-and-the-choice-of-small-immediate-over-larger-delayed-reward/); [Volkow 2011](https://www.nature.com/articles/mp201097)

### Inferences
- The defensible scientific version is: in ADHD, engagement depends more than usual on what is immediately rewarding, salient or pressing, and less on abstract or delayed importance. That partly overlaps with Dodson's language but does not prove a separate "interest-based" nervous system.
- For Marcelo's hypothesis, Dodson's claim would predict that importance (long-term goals) does not motivate. The science does not show that. It shows importance loses pull with delay, more on average in ADHD. Personally chosen, meaningful goals could still help if they are made immediate and concrete (the app's job), and if the task itself is made more engaging (novelty, short challenge, a clear finish line).
- Urgency: deadlines raise immediacy, which fits the app's "due soon" logic; but no peer-reviewed study was found that quantifies urgency as a driver in ADHD specifically.
- Evidence strength: the named construct has no direct peer-reviewed support; flag it as a popular claim in any report.

### Gaps
- No peer-reviewed study was found on novelty, challenge or competition as task-engagement drivers in adults with ADHD.
- No peer-reviewed study on personal "interest" as a moderator of task initiation in adult ADHD was found.

## 5. Evidence-based psychosocial treatments for adult ADHD that use planning structures (CBT, meta-cognitive therapy, DBT-based groups, college programs)

### Takeaway
CBT for adult ADHD has the best evidence of any psychosocial treatment: moderate-to-large effects against waitlist (SMD 0.76 to 1.03) that shrink to small-to-moderate against active controls (SMD 0.32 to 0.43); the core ingredients are planning skills (calendar, one task list, prioritizing, breaking tasks into steps), distractibility management and adaptive thinking about procrastination. Evidence strength: moderate-to-strong for CBT and Solanto's MCT; DBT-based group therapy did not beat individual clinical management in the largest trial.

### Cited Findings
**Key trials**
- Safren et al. (2010), "Cognitive behavioral therapy vs relaxation with educational support for medication-treated adults with ADHD and persistent symptoms: a randomized controlled trial," JAMA 304(8):875-880. N = 86 (43 per arm), adults already on medication with persistent symptoms, 12 individual sessions. Responders: 53% CBT vs 23% control on Clinical Global Impression; 67% vs 33% on the ADHD rating scale. CBT responders and partial responders kept gains at 6 and 12 months. A press release said 3 and 9 months, which conflicts with the JAMA-based sources. [EurekAlert summary](https://www.eurekalert.org/news-releases/632583); [Author manuscript, PMC3641654](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3641654); [EBSCO DynaMed summary](https://about.ebsco.com/clinical-decisions/dynamed-solutions/about/ebm-focus/individual-cbt-associated-sustained)
- Safren's program components (from the published manual "Mastering Your Adult ADHD," from prior knowledge, not re-verified in fetched text this session): psychoeducation; organization and planning (a single calendar and task list used daily, rating tasks by priority, problem solving, breaking overwhelming tasks into manageable steps); reducing distractibility (estimating attention span, breaking work into chunks of that length, a timer, a "distractibility delay" list); adaptive thinking (spotting and reframing thoughts that drive avoidance); optional modules on procrastination and relapse prevention. Source for the trial: [PMC3641654](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3641654). Verify component wording against the manual before quoting.
- Solanto et al. (2010), "Efficacy of meta-cognitive therapy for adult ADHD," American Journal of Psychiatry 167(8):958-968. 12-week manualized group therapy targeting time management, organization and planning. N = 88 clinically referred adults, stratified by medication and randomized to MCT or supportive therapy (an active control that gave support but no cognitive-behavioral strategies). Responder status strongly favored MCT (odds ratio 5.41, 95% CI 1.77-16.55) after controlling for baseline severity. Dropout 11% MCT vs 12% supportive therapy. A letter argued the program is cognitive-behavioral plus time management skills rather than truly "meta-cognitive." Funded by NIMH; Solanto disclosed industry advisory roles. [PubMed 20231319](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/20231319/); [Cochrane table, PMC6494390](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6494390/table/CD010840-tblf-0012); [Letter, AJP](https://ajp.psychiatryonline.org/doi/10.1176/appi.ajp.2010.10101467)
- Philipsen et al. (2015), COMPAS trial, "Effects of group psychotherapy, individual counseling, methylphenidate, and placebo in the treatment of adult ADHD: a randomized clinical trial," JAMA Psychiatry 72(12):1199-1210. 419 adults aged 18 to 58 (Germany), 2x2 design: DBT-based group psychotherapy vs individual clinical management, each with methylphenidate or placebo. Primary outcome Conners Adult ADHD Rating Scale ADHD Index: at 3 months group therapy 17.6 vs clinical management 16.5 (from baseline 20.6); group therapy was not superior. Methylphenidate beat placebo (16.2 vs 17.9, P = .003). A 1.5-year follow-up showed improvement held after 1 year of treatment. (per abstract and news summaries) [Healio](https://www.healio.com/news/psychiatry/20151105/methylphenidate-improved-outcomes-of-group-psychotherapy-individual-counseling-for-adult-adhd); [News-Medical](https://www.news-medical.net/news/20151109/Medication-not-psychotherapy-intensity-sways-treatment-outcome-in-adult-ADHD.aspx); [Ovid record](https://www.ovid.com/journals/japs/pdf/10.1001/jamapsychiatry.2015.2146~effects-of-group-psychotherapy-individual-counseling)

**College students**
- ACCESS (Accessing Campus Connections and Empowering Student Success), CBT for college students with ADHD: Anastopoulos, Langberg, Eddy, Silvia & Labban (2021), "A randomized controlled trial examining CBT for college students with ADHD," Journal of Consulting and Clinical Psychology 89(1):21-33. 250 students randomized to immediate treatment or delayed treatment (two semesters). Reported statistically and clinically significant improvements across multiple domains. 30 students assigned to immediate ACCESS could not attend the required group meetings (final analytic sample 119 immediate, 131 delayed; ClinicalTrials.gov lists enrollment 280). [IES award page](https://ies.ed.gov/use-work/awards/improving-educational-and-social-emotional-functioning-college-students-adhd); [ClinicalTrials.gov NCT04186312](https://clinicaltrials.gov/study/NCT04186312); [ACCESS project](https://accessproject.uncg.edu)
- ACCESS mechanisms paper (2023): in the same 250 students, improvement in adaptive thinking was small but strongly associated with outcomes. [GWU repository](https://hsrc.himmelfarb.gwu.edu/gwhpubs/2973)
- ACCESS open trial: 88 college students, Journal of Attention Disorders 24(6):863-874 (2020), examined maintenance of gains after active treatment. [ACCESS project](https://accessproject.uncg.edu/?p=508)

**Meta-analyses**
- Jensen, Amdisen, Jørgensen & Arnfred (2016), Journal of Attention Disorders, 9 RCTs: CBT vs waitlist SMD 0.76 (95% CI 0.21-1.31); CBT vs active control SMD 0.43 (95% CI 0.14-0.71). [PubMed 27554190](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/27554190/)
- Knouse, Teller & Brooks (2017), "Meta-analysis of cognitive-behavioral treatments for adult ADHD," Journal of Consulting and Clinical Psychology 85(7):737-750: 32 studies through Dec 2015; pre-post self-reported ADHD symptoms g = 1.00 (95% CI 0.84-1.16); authors judged effects comparable to behavioral treatments for children considered well established. [PubMed 28504540](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/28504540/)
- Solanto (2025), World Psychiatry, summary of 17 RCTs: effect sizes on core symptoms 1.03 vs waitlist, 0.66 vs treatment as usual, 0.32 vs active control; argues active controls are needed to isolate CBT's specific effect. [Wiley, World Psychiatry](https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/10.1002/wps.21349)
- A 2025 meta-analysis of 14 RCTs (Journal of Affective Disorders): core symptoms SMD -0.45, depressive mood SMD -0.23, anxiety/stress SMD -0.24. [ScienceDirect](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S0165032725025492)
- A meta-analytic review of long-term efficacy of psychosocial treatments for adult ADHD exists (López-Pinar et al. 2018); its numbers were not retrieved. [PMC5946687](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC5946687/)

### Inferences
- The tested treatments look a lot like Marcelo's WHAT and HOW layers: one calendar and one task list, prioritizing, breaking tasks into steps, managing distraction, and changing the thoughts that drive avoidance. His WHY layer (long-term chosen goals) is not a central, separately tested ingredient in these programs, as far as these sources show.
- The effect shrinkage against active controls (0.32 to 0.43) means a good part of the benefit comes from nonspecific factors (structure, regular contact, attention, expectation). An app gives structure but not human contact; this predicts smaller effects for an app alone than for therapist-led CBT.
- The ACCESS mechanisms result (adaptive thinking strongly linked to outcomes) suggests the "how I talk to myself about this task" piece matters, which fits the app's no-shame voice and the Pause button.
- The DBT-based group result shows that adding more therapy format does not automatically beat simpler structured support.
- Evidence strength: CBT for adults, moderate-to-strong (many RCTs, but small trials and inflated waitlist comparisons). MCT, moderate (one main RCT with an active control, strong odds ratio, wide CI). DBT group, weak as a stand-alone advantage. ACCESS for college students, moderate (one sizable RCT with delayed-treatment control, not an active control).

### Gaps
- Exact effect sizes (Cohen's d) for Safren 2010 and Solanto 2010 primary outcomes were not retrieved; only responder rates and odds ratios.
- Effect sizes for ACCESS (2021) were not retrieved.
- Whether goal-setting or values work (the WHY) is a component of any of these manuals, and whether it adds anything, was not found.
- Hirvikoski et al. (2011) DBT group pilot was not retrieved.

## 6. Coaching for college students with ADHD: what was measured and what improved?

### Takeaway
ADHD coaching studies in college students report gains in study and learning strategies (LASSI total and its Skill, Will and Self-Regulation subscales), self-esteem and well-being, sometimes with large effects (LASSI d about 1.0); but most studies are uncontrolled or quasi-experimental, often small, and several are linked to coaching providers. Evidence strength: preliminary, weaker than CBT.

### Cited Findings
- Field, Parker, Sawilowsky & Rolands (2013), "Assessing the impact of ADHD coaching services on university students' learning skills, self-regulation, and well-being," Journal of Postsecondary Education and Disability (the Edge Foundation study, Wayne State University). 127 students; treatment group n = 88 (Edge Foundation coaching), comparison n = 39, described as randomly assigned. Coached students scored significantly higher on LASSI total and all three LASSI factors (Skill, Will, Self-Regulation); well-being scores were significantly higher once initial differences in executive functioning were controlled. [Australian ADHD Clinical Practice Guideline, coaching page](https://adhdguideline.aadpa.com.au/non-%20pharmacological/adhd-coaching/); [Edge Foundation executive summary](https://adhd.org.sa/wp-content/uploads/2014/09/Edge-Research-Executive-Summary11_10.pdf); [ERIC record EJ1026813](https://files.eric.ed.gov/fulltext/EJ1026813.pdf)
- Prevatt & Yelland (2015), "An empirical evaluation of ADHD coaching in college students," Journal of Attention Disorders 19(8):666-677 (online 2013). 148 college students over 5 years in a university coaching program based on CBT and psychoeducation, with executive functioning used to frame problem areas and outcomes. Improvements in study and learning strategies (LASSI), self-esteem and symptom distress. Uncontrolled program evaluation; authors call for RCTs. [SAGE](https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/abs/10.1177/1087054713480036); [PubMed 23509112](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/23509112/)
- Prevatt, Lampropoulos, Bowles & Garrett (2011), "An examination of the effects of ADHD coaching on university students' executive functioning," Journal of Postsecondary Education and Disability: reports gains in executive functioning measures after coaching. (details not retrieved) [ERIC EJ943698](https://eric.ed.gov/?id=EJ943698)
- A randomized coaching study reported LASSI total d = 1.02 for coached students; a review notes LASSI effect sizes up to 1.10. The specific study behind d = 1.02 was not confirmed. [Evoke Learning summary](https://www.evokelearning.ca/blog/quantifying-the-effectiveness-of-coaching-for-college-students-with-attention-deficit-hyperactivity-disorder/); [Ahmann et al. descriptive review](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/A-Descriptive-Review-of-ADHD-Coaching-Research:-for-Ahmann-Tuttle/7d549e01929bee4f759705b4a75c3d82b25149db)
- A pilot RCT of coaching for college students with ADHD (East Carolina University thesis) and a study on comorbid anxiety and coaching response exist; one coaching pilot had only 7 undergraduates. [ECU pilot RCT](https://thescholarship.ecu.edu/handle/10342/5892); [ECU comorbid anxiety study](https://thescholarship.ecu.edu/server/api/core/bitstreams/3efacd30-470a-4919-aa5d-3c6b90483541/content)
- Prevatt & Levrini (2016), "Coaching for college students with ADHD," Current Psychiatry Reports 18:110: review of the field. [PubMed 27783338](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/27783338/); [Springer](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s11920-016-0751-9)
- Parker et al. (2013), "Self-control in postsecondary settings: students' perceptions of ADHD college coaching," Journal of Attention Disorders: qualitative study of students' views of coaching. [NOBCO knowledge base](https://nobco.nl/ik-ben-een-coach/ontwikkeling-inspiratie/kennisbank/self-control-postsecondary-settings-students-perceptions-adh)

### Inferences
- What improves in coaching studies is mostly self-reported study strategy and self-regulation (LASSI), plus well-being. Grades, GPA and objective task initiation were not reported as main outcomes in the sources found.
- Coaching typically combines goal-setting with weekly check-ins and accountability. The accountability and regular contact may be the active part, which an app alone does not provide; a "check-in" or "share with someone" feature (his fiancee, a friend) may matter more than the goal hierarchy itself. This is an inference, not a tested finding.
- Evidence strength: preliminary. One controlled study with an uneven comparison group (88 vs 39) and provider involvement (Edge Foundation funded and supplied coaches), plus uncontrolled program evaluations.

### Gaps
- The full Field et al. 2013 paper (ERIC) could not be fetched (host blocked), so randomization details, attrition and effect sizes are unverified. The AADPA guideline's formal recommendation on coaching was not retrieved.
- No coaching study found that measures task initiation or start latency directly.

## 7. How common is it that adults with ADHD fail to sustain a system (abandoning planners and apps)? Data on adherence to organizational tools

### Takeaway
There is no rigorous study of planner or app abandonment rates in adult ADHD; the best peer-reviewed evidence (a small HCI survey) finds adults with ADHD use calendars, to-do lists and planners about as often as others but rate them as less effective, and treatment studies show adherence to skill practice tracks outcome. Evidence strength: weak and indirect; the widely repeated statistics on abandonment are not verifiable.

### Cited Findings
- Desrochers, Tuson & Magee (2019), ASSETS 2019 conference paper (pp. 603-605, doi 10.1145/3308561.3354622): survey of adults with and without ADHD; adults with ADHD are dissatisfied with current digital time management tools. Later papers cite it as finding that calendars, to-do lists, planners and reminder systems are rated significantly less effective by adults with ADHD, even though both groups adopt and use them similarly. Sample size not retrieved. [Clark University record](https://commons.clarku.edu/faculty_computer_sciences/15); [arXiv 2026 paper citing it](https://arxiv.org/html/2603.17258v1)
- "Not Just Me and My To-Do List" (arXiv preprint, 2026): interviews with adults with ADHD on task management; participants describe many apps as too complicated to set up and some felt more anxious using them; argues for social scaffolds (other people) rather than solo tools. Preprint, not peer reviewed. [arXiv 2603.17258](https://arxiv.org/html/2603.17258v1)
- A real-world study of a psychoeducational smartphone app for adults with ADHD notes that low adherence and attrition may undermine app effectiveness. [PMC11847728](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC11847728/)
- Knouse & Safren describe a "double bind" in ADHD: the very skills, strategies and systems that would help manage symptoms are harder to use because of the symptoms. [Search summary of Knouse & Safren chapter, Univ. of Richmond](https://scholarship.richmond.edu/psychology-faculty-publications/42)
- Yovel & Safren (2007), Cognitive Therapy and Research, "Measuring homework utility in psychotherapy": proposed a within-person correlation of session-by-session homework adherence with symptom change, using adult ADHD CBT; homework utility was significantly associated with overall outcome. [Hebrew University record](https://cris.huji.ac.il/en/publications/measuring-homework-utility-in-psychotherapy-cognitive-behavioral/)
- Self-rated adherence to psychotherapy was associated with ADHD symptom improvement in an adult ADHD psychotherapy trial analysis (Psychotherapy and Psychosomatics; year and trial not confirmed from snippets). [Karger](https://karger.com/pps/article/doi/10.1159/000532043/880160/Exploring-the-Relationship-between-Adherence-to)
- A 2025 RCT of app-based CBT for adult ADHD found reductions in inattentive symptoms were associated with the total number of app exercises completed (dose-response, correlational). [SAGE, Journal of Attention Disorders 2025](https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/pdf/10.1177/10870547251384462)
- In ACCESS, 30 of the students randomized to immediate treatment could not attend the required group meetings, an example of attendance barriers in a college ADHD sample. [IES award page](https://ies.ed.gov/use-work/awards/improving-educational-and-social-emotional-functioning-college-students-adhd)
- Popular claim to treat with caution: a blog says a 2023 "Shimmer" survey of 1,200+ adults with ADHD found 68% had abandoned three or more digital planning tools and 71% of paper planner users kept their system 6+ months. No original survey, methods or peer-reviewed version was found. Do not cite as evidence. [docshelf blog](https://docshelf.org/blog/why-paper-planners-work-for-adhd-brains)

### Inferences
- Abandonment of planners is very likely common in ADHD (it follows from the "double bind" and from the attrition seen in app studies), but there is no reliable number. Any report should say "commonly reported, not well measured."
- Because use of the system predicts benefit (homework utility, app exercises completed), the most important design question for Marcelo's app may be whether he keeps using it, not which planning method it uses. Low setup cost, few steps and a calm return after a gap (already in the app) address the known failure point.
- His goals-first structure adds setup work (long-term goals, then weekly goals, then tasks). The evidence suggests that extra setup is a risk for adherence; making the goal layer optional or very light, and measuring whether he still uses it after 2 to 4 weeks, would test this directly.
- Evidence strength: weak. Small surveys, a preprint, correlational adherence data.

### Gaps
- No peer-reviewed data on how long adults with ADHD keep using a planner or task app, or on 30- and 90-day retention of ADHD productivity apps.
- No study comparing paper vs digital planners in ADHD was found.
- No study tests whether linking tasks to personally chosen long-term goals improves adherence to an organizational system in ADHD.
