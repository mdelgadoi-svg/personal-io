# Accountability and incentive designs for an ADHD planner: what helps, what backfires

Notes for the report writer. Evidence strength scale used throughout: **Strong** (several RCTs or a solid meta-analysis, replicated), **Moderate** (one good RCT or megastudy, or consistent field data), **Weak** (small, correlational, or self-selected data), **Very weak** (not peer reviewed, anecdote, or failed replication). Almost none of this research was done with people with ADHD; each section says how likely it is to transfer.

Research limits in this session: several hosts could not be fetched (pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov, faculty.wharton.upenn.edu, www.dominican.edu), so some details come from search-result abstracts and university write-ups rather than full texts. Items I recall from the literature but could not verify here are kept in the Gaps sections, marked "to verify".

## Overall: which incentives are good, which are bad, and what makes sense for his weekly goals

### Takeaway
The useful split is not "positive vs negative" but **task-focused, forgiving, process-based and immediate** (tends to help) vs **self-focused, all-or-nothing, outcome-based and delayed** (tends to backfire). Some "negative" designs (loss-framed points, deposits) do work in the short run for people without ADHD, but their gains usually vanish when the incentive stops, many people who sign up lose money, and they rebuild the fear-and-guilt loop he wants to leave.

### Cited Findings
- Loss framing works, briefly: in an RCT of 281 adults, losing $1.40 from a pre-paid monthly pot for each missed 7,000-step day raised the share of goal days by 16 percentage points over control (95% CI 0.06 to 0.26); the same money framed as a gain did nothing, and no group kept the effect after incentives stopped. Strength: Moderate. [Patel et al. 2016, Annals of Internal Medicine, via CHIBE](https://chibe.upenn.edu/publications/framing-financial-incentives-to-increase-physical-activity-among-overweight-and-obese-adults-a-randomized-controlled-trial/); [Penn Medicine news](https://www.pennmedicine.org/news/news-releases/2016/february/to-encourage-physical-activity)
- Rewarding a comeback is the single best design found in a 61,293-person megastudy of 54 gym programs: a small bonus for returning after a missed workout raised visits 27%. Only 8% of the 54 programs still changed behavior after the 4 weeks ended. Strength: Moderate to Strong (huge sample, one setting). [Milkman et al. 2021, Nature, via Penn Today](https://penntoday.upenn.edu/news/wharton-study-best-ways-boost-workout-habits); [Caltech record](https://authors.library.caltech.edu/records/mys88-cc756)
- Feedback raises performance on average (d = 0.41) but more than a third of feedback interventions make performance worse, mostly when the feedback draws attention to the self instead of the task. Strength: Strong. [Kluger & DeNisi 1996, Psychological Bulletin](https://albany.edu/faculty/ts460/pad634/Files/2361-KlugerDeNisiFeedbackcapHighlighted.pdf)
- Process accountability (being answerable for how you went about it) tends to increase completion of target behaviors; outcome accountability "has primarily detrimental effects", according to the supportive accountability model. Strength: Moderate for the underlying lab literature, Weak for the model as applied to apps (it is a set of untested hypotheses). [Mohr, Cuijpers & Lehman 2011, JMIR](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3221353)
- Showing a broken streak lowers later engagement compared with an intact one, even when actual past behavior is identical; the harm is larger when people blame themselves and smaller when the streak can be repaired. Strength: Moderate (7 studies, one paper). [Silverman & Barasch 2023, Journal of Consumer Research](https://udspace.udel.edu/items/42ce576b-8e1f-429a-8541-e29e48dbcfbb)

### Inferences
- **Use:** weekly goals framed as a fresh start each Monday; accountability for process ("did I plan it, did I start it") rather than outcome ("did I finish"); a warm welcome and a small bonus for coming back after a missed day or week; immediate, task-focused feedback; temptation bundling done properly (pleasure only during the work); small visible progress on work that matters to him.
- **Use with care:** loss-framed points that reset every week (STEP UP style, see gamification section); a human accountability partner, only if the person is seen as warm and expert and the check-in is about process; competition, only with a willing partner.
- **Avoid:** streaks, cumulative scores that can be "broken", red marks, money at stake, outcome-only weekly scorecards, public announcements of identity goals, self-focused messages ("you failed", "you are behind"), and nudges so frequent they become noise.
- The app's existing choices (no streaks, no points, "Not yet" instead of red, calm return after days away) are consistent with this evidence. The new weekly accountability should keep the same rules: what gets counted is starting and planning, and a missed week ends in a fresh start, not a debt.
- On his framing: goal-based motivation is not automatically "positive". A weekly goal that is scored pass/fail becomes outcome accountability and can bring back guilt. What makes it positive is how a miss is handled.

### Gaps
- No study tested any of these incentive designs in adults with ADHD in a planner app. All transfer claims are reasoned, not measured.

## Accountability to another person, accountability partners, public commitment, and the Gail Matthews study

### Takeaway
Accountability helps only under specific conditions (an audience whose views you do not know, who cares about process and accuracy, and who you feel accountable to before you act). The often quoted Dominican University goals study by Gail Matthews was never published in a peer-reviewed journal and is weak evidence. For digital tools, the best framework is "supportive accountability": a warm, trusted, expert person checking process, not outcomes.

### Cited Findings
- Lerner & Tetlock 1999 is a review (not a meta-analysis) in Psychological Bulletin 125(2):255-275 of the accountability literature. Later papers cite it for the conclusion that accountability sometimes has no effect on judgment, sometimes increases bias and sometimes reduces it, and for the distinction between process and outcome accountability; accountability for accuracy is linked to more careful processing. Strength: Strong as a review of lab studies. [ScienceOpen record](https://scienceopen.com/document/vid/8b078b30-781e-4506-a70f-8a2818cd26cb); [Cambridge JDM paper citing it](https://www.cambridge.org/core/services/aop-cambridge-core/content/view/A16B2380B817354B0F39A392BFD1FD5C/S1930297500006732a.pdf/accountability-and-adaptive-performance-under-uncertainty-a-long-term-view.pdf)
- The review's abstract (wording recalled, the full text could not be fetched in this session; verify) says self-critical, effortful thinking is most likely when people learn before forming any opinion that they will be accountable to an audience (a) whose views are unknown, (b) who is interested in accuracy, (c) who is interested in processes rather than specific outcomes, (d) who is reasonably well informed, and (e) who has a legitimate reason to ask. Accountability after a decision tends to produce self-justification, and a known audience view produces conformity. [Lerner & Tetlock 1999, DOI](https://doi.org/10.1037/0033-2909.125.2.255)
- Supportive accountability model: human support improves adherence to eHealth programs through accountability to a coach seen as trustworthy, benevolent and expert; expectations should be clear, process-oriented and set together with the patient; the more intrinsically motivated the person, the less support needed; the communication channel (phone, chat, email) changes the effect. The authors present each part as a testable hypothesis, not a proven result. Strength: Weak to Moderate (theory built on reviews of guided vs unguided internet treatment). [Mohr, Cuijpers & Lehman 2011, JMIR 13(1):e30](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3221353); [JMIR page](https://jmir.org/2011/1/e30/authors)
- Gail Matthews (Dominican University of California) study: participants were randomly assigned to 5 groups: unwritten goals; written goals; written goals plus action commitments; those plus commitments sent to a friend; those plus weekly progress reports to a friend. The weekly-report group accomplished significantly more than the commitment-to-a-friend group, which did better than the written-action and unwritten groups. Results exist as a research summary and a conference presentation, not a journal article. Strength: Very weak. [Dominican research summary PDF](https://www.dominican.edu/sites/default/files/2020-02/gailmatthews-harvard-goals-researchsummary.pdf); [Dominican Scholar, listed under faculty conference presentations](https://scholar.dominican.edu/psychology-faculty-conference-presentations/3/)
- Sample size for the Matthews study is reported inconsistently: a 2010 Daily Bruin article says 149 participants in five groups; later write-ups say 267 participants from around the world. Goal accomplishment was self-rated. [Daily Bruin 2010](https://dailybruin.com/2010/10/14/students_can_benefit_from_goalsetting); [Inc. coverage](https://www.inc.com/marla-tabaka/this-study-found-1-simple-step-to-practically-guarantee-youll-achieve-your-goals-for-real.html)
- Matthews ran the study partly in response to the "Harvard/Yale written goals study", which turned out to be an urban myth. [Goodreads author blog post on the myth](https://www.goodreads.com/author_blog_posts/9071103-the-harvard-and-yale-goals-studies-a-myth-but?tab=book)
- In stickK's public data, only a minority of commitments name an outside referee (about 19%), but naming one is associated with higher self-reported success. This is self-selected and correlational. Strength: Weak. [ICWSM paper on stickK data](https://ojs.aaai.org/index.php/ICWSM/article/download/31440/33600)
- ADHD coaching (which builds in regular accountability check-ins): a 5-year evaluation of 148 college students with ADHD who took an 8-week coaching program found improvement on all 10 study and learning scales, self-esteem, symptom distress and satisfaction with school and work, but there was no control group and the authors call for RCTs. A 20-student pilot RCT was inconclusive, trending in the expected direction. Strength: Weak. [Prevatt & Yelland 2015, Journal of Attention Disorders, summary](https://nobco.nl/ik-ben-een-coach/ontwikkeling-inspiratie/kennisbank/empirical-evaluation-adhd-coaching-college-students); [ECU pilot RCT](https://thescholarship.ecu.edu/handle/10342/5892)

### Inferences
- A weekly check-in should ask about process ("Which goal did you plan? What did you start?") and be set by him in advance. This matches both Lerner & Tetlock's conditions and Mohr's model.
- An app alone cannot provide the "trustworthy, benevolent, expert person" in Mohr's model. If he wants human accountability, a coach or a person he sees as warm and non-judging fits the model; a partner who already knows his views and whose disapproval he fears (for example a family figure from the guilt years) fits poorly. His fiancee could work if the check-in is about process and she is not the judge of outcomes; this is a judgment call, not a tested claim.
- The Matthews study should not be cited as proof. It only suggests, weakly, that weekly progress reports to someone may help.
- Transfer to ADHD: plausible. Coaching with regular check-ins is a common ADHD support, but its evidence base is weak.

### Gaps
- Public commitment can backfire: Gollwitzer et al. 2009 ("When intentions go public", Psychological Science) reportedly found that telling others an identity-related intention reduced later effort, because the announcement already felt like progress. To verify; I could not retrieve it in this session. Later replications of this effect are also not checked.
- Field et al. 2013 and Parker et al. coaching studies for students with ADHD could not be verified.
- No study found on accountability partners (peer-to-peer) as a stand-alone intervention with good controls.

## Body doubling for ADHD

### Takeaway
Body doubling (working with someone else present, in person or online) is widely used and liked in the ADHD community, but peer-reviewed outcome evidence is close to nil: one small 2025 EEG study found no significant effect, and the rest are surveys, design studies or preprints. Strength: Very weak.

### Cited Findings
- A peer-reviewed ACM ASSETS 2025 paper measured body doubling with EEG and found no statistically significant differences between body doubling and working alone, no measurable harm, and "promising trends" for unmedicated participants with ADHD; the sample was small (reported as about 26). [Schuenke, Dickenson & Moore 2025, ACM ASSETS](https://dl.acm.org/doi/10.1145/3663547.3759743)
- A 2025 virtual reality study compared no body double, a human body double and an AI body double in adults with ADHD; participants reported better accuracy and sustained attention with a body double. It is an arXiv preprint, not peer reviewed. [Ara et al. 2025, arXiv](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2509.12153)
- A 2026 roadmap paper describes research on body doubling as very recent and limited in number. [Mixed Reality Body Doubling roadmap, arXiv](https://arxiv.org/html/2605.07851)

### Inferences
- A simple "work with me" feature (for example a shared calendar block with a friend, or a video co-working link) costs little and does no measured harm, but there is no evidence it raises output. It also needs other people, which the app's no-server design cannot provide except through outside tools.
- His built-in games are the opposite of body doubling (solo, at home). That is fine; they serve a different purpose.

### Gaps
- A 2024 survey by Eagle et al. of about 220 neurodivergent adults on how they use body doubling was mentioned in search summaries but I could not retrieve it; it reportedly measured use, not outcomes.
- The classic social facilitation meta-analysis (Bond & Titus 1983, Psychological Bulletin, 241 studies) reportedly found that the presence of others slightly speeds simple tasks and slightly impairs complex ones, with very small effects. To verify; not retrieved here.
- No RCT of body doubling or virtual co-working on task completion exists that I could find.

## Commitment devices: self-imposed deadlines, deposits, stickK

### Takeaway
Commitment devices help a minority who choose them and are realistic about their own self-control. Take-up is low (about 1 in 10 when offered), many takers fail and lose money, and the best known deadline study failed to replicate in 2026. For a person with ADHD, the risk of repeated loss, shame and quitting is high. Strength: Moderate that they help some takers; Moderate that they often harm the people who over-commit.

### Cited Findings
- Ariely & Wertenbroch 2002 (Psychological Science 13(3):219-224): students chose costly self-imposed deadlines; these helped compared with no deadlines, but were not set optimally and worked worse than evenly spaced external deadlines. The proofreading study paid about 60 students to proofread three passages. [PubMed record](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/12009041/); [APS summary](https://psychologicalscience.org/?p=97145)
- A 2026 close replication of the proofreading study in Psychological Science reported that its results do not replicate the original; earlier economics experiments had also found deadlines less effective than claimed, including one where regular deadlines for a long task neither reduced procrastination nor improved performance. Strength of the original claim now: Weak. [2026 replication, Psychological Science](https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/full/10.1177/09567976261460772); [University of Hawaii WP 10-12](https://www.economics.hawaii.edu/research/workingpapers/WP_10-12.pdf)
- CARES smoking commitment account (Philippines): about 11% of smokers offered the account took it; being offered it raised the chance of passing a surprise urine test at 12 months by 3.4 to 5.7 percentage points. The authors could not separate the effect of the money from the weekly visits by bank staff collecting deposits. Strength: Moderate (RCT). [Giné, Karlan & Zinman 2010, AEJ Applied, via IPA](https://es.poverty-action.org/sites/default/files/publications/put_your_money_where_your_butt_is.pdf); [J-PAL summary](https://www.povertyactionlab.org/print/pdf/node/1468)
- "When Commitment Fails" (Philippines savings field experiment): people who were only partly aware of their self-control problems picked weak commitments and then defaulted; a significant share took the contract by mistake, and present bias predicted both take-up and default. [John 2020, Management Science](https://pubsonline.informs.org/doi/fpi/10.1287/mnsc.2018.3236); [Yale working paper](https://economics.yale.edu/sites/default/files/john_when_commitment_fails_march2018.pdf)
- In a hypertension study in rural India, take-up was high but many people lost money with no health gain, and the authors judged commitment devices possibly welfare reducing; in an HIV testing study in Malawi, 59% of those given a commitment device lost their stake. [Derksen et al., arXiv, summarizing Bai et al. 2020 and its own results](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2110.06876)
- stickK company data (125,000 contracts, self-selected, not independently checked) is reported as about 29% success with no referee or money, up to about 80% with money at stake; other reports of the same data give 59% with a referee and 71.5% with money. Academic analyses of stickK data find money stakes and anti-charity stakes associated with higher success, but most users add neither. Strength: Weak (selection bias: people who put money down differ). [HBS Working Knowledge](https://www.library.hbs.edu/working-knowledge/the-business-of-behavioral-economics); [Good Medicine blog](https://www.goodmedicine.org.uk/blog/commitment-contracts-another-good-way-of-helping-us-reach-our-goals); [KAIST 2021 analysis of 75,828 commitments](https://ic.kaist.ac.kr/files/papers/lee2021sticky.pdf); [ICWSM paper](https://ojs.aaai.org/index.php/ICWSM/article/download/31440/33600)

### Inferences
- People with ADHD often plan as if their future self will have more focus than it does. That is exactly the "partial sophistication" that John 2020 links to weak commitments followed by default and loss. Money-at-stake contracts are likely a poor fit and would recreate guilt.
- A soft commitment device fits better: he picks this week's goal and its first time slot on Monday, written to his calendar, with no penalty for missing it. This keeps the "decide in advance" part, drops the punishment.
- Transfer to ADHD: low confidence. No commitment device trial in ADHD was found.

### Gaps
- Bryan, Karlan & Nelson 2010 ("Commitment devices", Annual Review of Economics) was not retrieved; its framing of soft vs hard commitments and low take-up should be checked directly.
- The share of CARES takers who failed and forfeited their deposit was not confirmed in this session.

## Temptation bundling (and why his games are not quite that)

### Takeaway
Temptation bundling means allowing a guilty pleasure only while doing the "should" task. It worked in a 2014 field experiment (big early lift that faded) and modestly in a larger 2020 replication, where most of the effect came from simply giving people the pleasure, with small extra gain from teaching the bundle. Strength: Moderate for a small effect.

### Cited Findings
- Original study: audiobooks only available at the gym raised gym visits 51% at first, and the effect faded over time. [Milkman, Minson & Volpp 2014, Management Science](https://pubsonline.informs.org/doi/fpi/10.1287/mnsc.2013.1784); [PubMed Central full text](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4381662/)
- Larger replication with members of a fitness chain: giving a free audiobook raised gym visits even without any instruction; adding encouragement to bundle it with exercise had a modest extra positive effect, mainly on whether people went at all. One write-up reports a 10% to 14% higher chance of a weekly visit and 10% to 12% more weekly visits, lasting up to 17 weeks after a 4-week program. Strength: Moderate; the exact figures come from secondary sources. [Kirgios et al. 2020, OBHDP, Penn repository](https://repository.upenn.edu/handle/20.500.14332/37902); [Rotman Management, Kirgios](https://www.pressreader.com/canada/rotman-management-magazine/20210501/282973927858878); [Catalog abstract](https://katalog.lib.cas.cz/KNAV/EdsRecord/edsair,edsair.doi...........ed605f827a44033dfe03046389d2643e)

### Inferences
- His Procrastination section is **not** temptation bundling: chess and Sudoku are played instead of or between tasks, not during them. Playing first and then being shown the next task is closer to "earn it later" or a planned break.
- A real bundle in his app would be a pleasure tied to the work itself (a playlist, podcast or favorite cafe that he only uses while doing a disliked task). This could be offered as one optional line on the start screen, but it is new scope.
- The Follower (games get harder when a task is near) has no direct evidence for or against it. It is a reasonable, untested design and his own measures (play minutes, starts within 10 minutes after play) are the right test.
- Transfer to ADHD: plausible. Preference for immediate rewards is stronger in ADHD (see next section), so making the hard task immediately pleasant should help at least as much as in the general population.

### Gaps
- Whether the Nature megastudy's temptation-bundling arms worked was not checked.
- No ADHD study of temptation bundling found.

## Rewards, the overjustification effect, and immediate rewards in ADHD

### Takeaway
Expected tangible rewards for doing an interesting task reduce later interest in it (Deci, Koestner & Ryan 1999), but praise and informational feedback do not. People with ADHD prefer small immediate rewards over larger delayed ones more than others do, and behavioral treatments built on frequent, immediate rewards work well in children. For an adult planner, the lesson is: make progress visible right away, keep rewards informational, and do not pay himself for work he already cares about.

### Cited Findings
- Meta-analysis of 128 experiments: engagement-contingent, completion-contingent and performance-contingent rewards significantly lowered free-choice intrinsic motivation (d = -0.40, -0.36 and -0.28). Strength: Strong (but see the critic below). [Deci, Koestner & Ryan 1999, Psychological Bulletin 125(6):627-668](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/10589297/); [full text PDF](https://depts.washington.edu/techdocs/papers/deciExtrinsicRewardsAndIntrinsicMotivation99.pdf)
- The same authors note that verbal rewards can also have a controlling side when people act to get praise. [Deci, Koestner & Ryan 2001, Review of Educational Research](https://www.selfdeterminationtheory.org/SDT/documents/2001_DeciKoestnerRyan.pdf)
- A rival meta-analysis (Cameron & Pierce 1994) concluded the undermining effect is minimal for practical purposes; the debate is real. [Cited in search record](https://link.springer.com/doi/10.1007/BF03392017)
- ADHD and immediate rewards: a meta-analysis of 37 group comparisons (3,763 participants) found small-to-medium effects: people with ADHD choose small immediate over larger delayed rewards more often than controls; real rewards almost doubled the odds ratio compared with hypothetical ones. [Marx et al. 2021, Journal of Attention Disorders](https://searchworks.jioinstitute.edu.in/articles/cmedm__29806533)
- Delay aversion as an ADHD pathway, moderate effect for the combined presentation (r = 0.34, 95% CI 0.19 to 0.49); evidence on whether this weakens with age is mixed. [Schulze et al. 2021, Frontiers in Psychiatry](https://www.frontiersin.org/articles/10.3389/fpsyt.2021.519840/full)
- Behavioral treatments for ADHD (which rely on contingency management, such as token systems and daily report cards): meta-analysis of 174 studies, between-group effect size about 0.83; only 20 of the 174 used between-group designs, so the larger within-subject numbers are likely inflated. Mostly children. Strength: Strong for children. [Fabiano et al. 2009, Clinical Psychology Review](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/19131150/); [critique noting design mix](https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/full/10.1177/1087054712444732)

### Inferences
- For an adult with ADHD, what transfers best from token economies is **immediacy and frequency** (something good right after starting), not points that pile up. The app's "Start 1 minute" and immediate "Done" check already give a fast, small win.
- Paying himself (money, points) for work tied to his life goals risks the undermining effect, because those goals are meant to be intrinsically important. Using games as a reward is less risky because it is a break, not a payment, and games are not "contingent" on output in his design.
- Informational feedback ("you started 3 of your 4 planned things this week") is safer than controlling feedback ("you must reach 5").
- Transfer to ADHD: High for "immediate beats delayed". Low to moderate for token economies in adults (evidence is mostly in children with parents or teachers running the system).

### Gaps
- To verify: the 1999 abstract reportedly also says tangible rewards overall undermined motivation (about d = -0.34), unexpected and task-noncontingent rewards did not, and verbal rewards (positive feedback) increased intrinsic motivation in college students but not in children; the paper also reportedly found no undermining for boring tasks. Not confirmed in this session.
- No RCT of token economies or self-administered rewards in adults with ADHD was found.

## Gamification, streaks, points, and loss-framed incentives

### Takeaway
Gamification has small positive effects on average, strongest when it includes social interaction or story, and weak and unstable for behavior. Loss-framed weekly points with a weekly reset plus competition raised steps in a large RCT, but only competition lasted. Streaks are a clear risk: a broken streak lowers later engagement, more so when people blame themselves. Strength: Moderate overall.

### Cited Findings
- Meta-analysis of gamification in learning: small positive effects on cognitive (g = 0.49, k = 19, N = 1,686), motivational (g = 0.36, k = 16, N = 2,246) and behavioral outcomes (g = 0.25, k = 9, N = 951, figure from a secondary source); behavioral effects were less stable; game fiction and social interaction moderated behavior effects; the analysis was likely underpowered and publication bias was not tested. [Sailer & Homner 2020, Educational Psychology Review](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s10648-019-09498-w); [open PDF](https://opus.bibliothek.uni-augsburg.de/opus4/files/109056/109056.pdf); [secondary source for behavioral g](https://elearningindustry.com/gamification-works-just-not-the-way-most-courses-use-it)
- STEP UP RCT (602 adults with overweight or obesity, 40 US states, 24 weeks): each week players got 70 points and lost 10 for every day they missed their step goal, with levels up or down at week's end. All three game types (support, collaboration, competition) raised steps; competition was best (+920 steps per day vs control) and was the only one still ahead after the game ended (+569). Funded by Deloitte Consulting and the Penn Nudge Unit. Strength: Moderate. [Patel et al. 2019, JAMA Internal Medicine](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6735420); [ACC summary](https://www.acc.org/latest-in-cardiology/articles/2019/11/26/08/23/key-takeaways-regarding-gamification-to-increase-physical-activity)
- Loss-framed money (Patel 2016, 281 adults): +16 percentage points of goal days vs control; gain framing no better than control; no lasting effect in any arm after incentives stopped; mean daily steps difference not significant (861, P = 0.056). [CHIBE publication page](https://chibe.upenn.edu/publications/framing-financial-incentives-to-increase-physical-activity-among-overweight-and-obese-adults-a-randomized-controlled-trial/); [Penn Today](https://penntoday.upenn.edu/news/encourage-physical-activity-potential-lose-financial-reward-more-effective-gaining-one-penn-stu)
- Streaks: across 7 studies, an intact logged streak raised later engagement compared with a broken one, independent of actual past behavior; people treat keeping the streak as a goal in itself; the drop is larger when people blame themselves for the break and smaller when they can "repair" the streak; in one study people switched to a new language rather than restart a broken streak in the same one. [Silverman & Barasch 2023, JCR 49(6):1095-1117](https://udspace.udel.edu/items/42ce576b-8e1f-429a-8541-e29e48dbcfbb); [CU Boulder news](https://www.colorado.edu/business/news/2023/04/20/research-streaks-marketing-tech-barasch); [Psychology Today summary](https://www.psychologytoday.com/us/blog/ulterior-motives/202306/how-broken-streaks-sap-motivation)
- "What-the-hell" effect: dieters who break a rule often give up the goal entirely and indulge (Polivy & Herman 1985; Cochran & Tesser 1996 chapter on goal framing); some later evidence suggests the effect in dieting may be weaker than first thought. Strength: Moderate for the phenomenon, Weak for its size. [Burnette & Finkel 2012, JESP, describing the effect](https://faculty.wcas.northwestern.edu/eli-finkel/documents/2012_BurnetteFinkel_JESP.pdf); [EFPSA research summary](https://more.efpsa.org/rpblog/?p=1172)

### Inferences
- The app's no-streak rule is well supported. A streak would turn a single bad day (common in ADHD) into a self-blamed break, the exact condition where the harm is largest.
- If he wants something game-like for weekly goals, the least risky form is STEP UP's structure without the shame: a fresh weekly count that starts at zero, counts starts (not misses), and never carries a "broken" record into the next week.
- Loss framing is the honest "negative incentive that works" in this literature, but in both Patel trials the effects were modest and short-lived, and his history with fear and guilt is a strong reason not to use it.
- Competition worked best in STEP UP, but needs another person and a server. Not a fit now.
- Transfer to ADHD: moderate. Novelty-seeking may make gamification work faster at first and fade faster; not measured.

### Gaps
- No gamification meta-analysis specific to ADHD adults or to productivity apps was found.
- Effect sizes for how much a broken streak lowers engagement were not extracted.

## Fresh start effect: Mondays, new periods, coming back after a bad week

### Takeaway
People are more likely to start goal behavior right after time landmarks (new week, month, semester, birthday). The best intervention in a 61,000-person megastudy rewarded people for coming back after a miss. Both support "weekly goals that restart every Monday" and "a warm, rewarded return after a bad week". Strength: Moderate (field data, consistent).

### Cited Findings
- Three archival field studies (Google searches for "diet", university gym attendance, stickK commitments) showed more goal behavior after temporal landmarks. Published in Management Science 60(10):2563-2582. [Dai, Milkman & Riis 2014, EconPapers](https://econpapers.repec.org/RePEc:inm:ormnsc:v:60:y:2014:i:10:p:2563-2582); [UCLA Anderson copy](https://anderson-review.ucla.edu/wp-content/uploads/2021/03/Dai-Milkman-Riis_FreshStartEffect_2014_Mgmt_Sci.pdf)
- Gym visits rose at the start of a new week, month, year and semester and after birthdays (21st birthdays were an exception). [Daily Pennsylvanian](https://www.thedp.com/article/2014/01/study-demystifies-how-people-keep-new-years-resolutions); [Wharton Knowledge](https://knowledge.wharton.upenn.edu/article/need-fresh-start-heres-begin/)
- Secondary figures (not confirmed against the paper): about 12,000 students' gym records; attendance up about 33% at the start of a week and about 47% at the start of a semester, and about 7.5% after a birthday; more diet searches and goal contracts on Mondays. [Silicon Canals write-up](https://siliconcanals.com/?p=104129)
- Megastudy: the top program sent members who missed a planned workout a small bonus (125 points, about $0.09) if they came back for their next planned workout, raising gym visits 27%. [Penn Today](https://penntoday.upenn.edu/news/wharton-study-best-ways-boost-workout-habits); [Scimex](https://www.scimex.org/newsfeed/which-programs-give-gym-goers-the-biggest-boost)

### Inferences
- Weekly goals fit the fresh start effect if each week truly starts clean: Monday shows this week's goal and its first small step, never last week's shortfall.
- After a bad week, the app should treat return as the win (consistent with the existing calm-return welcome). The megastudy result suggests a small positive moment for coming back is the strongest single lever found among 54 tested designs.
- Fresh starts can also be offered on other landmarks he cares about (start of a semester, after exams, the first of the month), not only Mondays.
- Transfer to ADHD: plausible to strong. People with ADHD often have more "bad weeks", so a reliable restart point matters more, not less.

### Gaps
- To verify: Dai, Milkman & Riis 2015 (Psychological Science) reportedly showed that describing a date as a "new beginning" increases intent to start goals; not retrieved.
- Fresh start effects fade if landmarks come too often; no study on how often a "fresh start" can be offered before it loses force was found.

## Feedback and self-monitoring

### Takeaway
Feedback helps on average, but over a third of feedback interventions lower performance, especially feedback that points at the person rather than the task. Feedback for him should be about the task and the next step, never about his worth. Strength: Strong.

### Cited Findings
- Meta-analysis of 607 effect sizes (23,663 observations): feedback interventions improved performance on average (d = 0.41), but more than a third decreased it; the negative cases were not explained by sampling error or by whether the feedback was positive or negative. Feedback Intervention Theory says effectiveness drops as attention moves from the task toward the self. [Kluger & DeNisi 1996, Psychological Bulletin 119(2):254-284](https://albany.edu/faculty/ts460/pad634/Files/2361-KlugerDeNisiFeedbackcapHighlighted.pdf); [abstract, Hebrew University](https://scholars.huji.ac.il/node/1802)
- Setbacks weigh more than progress: in the Amabile and Kramer diaries, negative events affected inner work life about 2 to 3 times more strongly than progress (secondary report). [Goodreads Q&A with Amabile](https://www.goodreads.com/author_blog_posts/1449704-why-progress-matters-6-questions-for-harvard-s-teresa-amabile)

### Inferences
- Weekly feedback should name tasks ("You started the thesis outline twice this week") and offer the next step, not a grade ("You hit 40% of your goals").
- Showing what did not happen is the riskiest kind of feedback for someone with a guilt history; "Not yet" is the right voice.
- Self-monitoring of play minutes (already in Settings) is informational and private, which is the safer form.
- Transfer to ADHD: high. The finding is general and comes from many populations.

### Gaps
- A meta-analysis specific to self-monitoring in ADHD adults was not searched in this session.

## Small wins and the progress principle

### Takeaway
Making progress in meaningful work was the strongest everyday booster of mood and motivation in a large diary study, but the evidence is correlational, mostly presented in a book and HBR article, and not a controlled test. Strength: Weak to Moderate.

### Cited Findings
- About 12,000 daily diary entries (dataset lists 11,637, 75% response rate) from 238 professionals at 7 companies (one source says 26 project teams); progress in meaningful work was the strongest factor in a good "inner work life"; progress on work people did not care about did not reliably help. [HBS Working Knowledge](https://www.library.hbs.edu/working-knowledge/how-small-wins-unleash-creativity); [Harvard Dataverse record](https://dx.doi.org/10.7910/DVN/25463); [Progress Principle site](https://progressprinciple.com/progress-principle/)

### Inferences
- The idea fits his app: one small visible win early in the day (the first small step and the "Done" check). It also supports linking each task to a goal with a "why", because progress only helped when the work felt meaningful.
- Because the data are correlational, it is unclear whether progress causes good days or good days cause progress. Do not oversell it.
- Transfer to ADHD: plausible; small, quick wins also match the immediate-reward finding in ADHD.

### Gaps
- Peer-reviewed papers from the same diary dataset were not checked in this session.

## Nudges, reminder frequency, alert fatigue, and habit formation

### Takeaway
Notifications work strongly in the short term (people open an app far more in the hour after one), but frequent fixed prompts risk habituation. Habits take a long and variable time to form (average 66 days, very wide range) and a single missed day does not break the process. Strength: Moderate.

### Cited Findings
- Micro-randomized trial in the Drink Less app (350 users, 30 days): receiving a notification increased the chance of opening the app in the next hour 3.5-fold (95% CI 2.91 to 4.25); this effect did not change significantly over the 30 days; being already engaged lowered the effect, not significantly. The authors had hypothesized some users would habituate to daily prompts. [Bell et al. 2023, JMIR mHealth and uHealth](https://mhealth.jmir.org/2023/1/e38342); [PMC full text](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10337295)
- Methodological reviews define habituation as reduced responsiveness after frequent exposure, to the point that a person may stop noticing the prompt. [Same paper and references](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10337295)
- Habit formation (96 recruited, 82 completed, 84 days of daily self-reports): average time to reach near-peak automaticity was 66 days, ranging from 18 to a projected 254 days; missing one opportunity did not significantly affect habit formation, but very inconsistent performers did not form habits. The 66-day average applies only to participants the model fit well. [UCL news on Lally et al. 2010](https://www.ucl.ac.uk/news/2009/aug/how-long-does-it-take-form-habit); [BPS Research Digest](https://bps.org.uk/research-digest/how-form-habit); [UCL Behavioural Science and Health blog](https://blogs.ucl.ac.uk/bsh/2012/06/29)
- The popular "21 days to a habit" figure traces to Maxwell Maltz's 1960 observations of surgery patients, not to research. [Irish Times](https://www.irishtimes.com/science/2025/05/01/forming-useful-habits-not-as-easy-as-widely-believed)

### Inferences
- A few well-timed nudges (at the start of a planned task, at 25 and 45 minutes of play) are in line with the evidence. Daily fixed nudges at the same time are the type most at risk of being tuned out; varying the wording and tying nudges to real events (a task starting) is safer.
- Habit evidence supports the app's forgiving design: missing a day is normal and harmless; what matters is a stable cue (same time or same trigger) repeated over about two months. A weekly review on the same day and time is a good candidate habit.
- Transfer to ADHD: plausible but uncertain. People with ADHD may need more external cues for longer, and may also habituate faster to repeated alerts; neither was measured here.

### Gaps
- To verify: HeartSteps micro-randomized trial (Klasnja et al. 2019) reportedly found that activity suggestions raised steps in the next 30 minutes, with the effect shrinking over days in the study; this would be direct evidence for habituation. Not retrieved.
- Gardner, Lally & Wardle 2012 ("Making health habitual", British Journal of General Practice) on context-dependent repetition was not retrieved.
- No study on alert fatigue in ADHD adults using planner apps was found.

## Weekly reviews and structured reflection

### Takeaway
Short, structured reflection on what one learned improved performance in a field experiment at a call-center training program (about 23%), through better understanding and higher confidence. It supports a short weekly review, framed as learning rather than judgment. Strength: Weak to Moderate (one field experiment plus lab studies, working paper, see caveat).

### Cited Findings
- In a field experiment at Wipro's tech support training in Bangalore, workers asked to reflect in writing on what they had learned scored 22.8% higher on the final training test than the control group; the authors attribute the effect to better task understanding and higher self-efficacy. The paper is a working paper, revised on SSRN in 2023. [HBS Working Knowledge](https://www.library.hbs.edu/working-knowledge/reflecting-on-work-improves-job-performance); [SSRN, Di Stefano, Gino, Pisano & Staats](https://papers.ssrn.com/abstract=2414478); [HAL record](https://ideas.repec.org/p/hal/wpaper/hal-01970756.html)

### Inferences
- A weekly review fits the evidence if it asks "What worked? What will I try next week?" and ends with choosing next week's goal and first step (one small action, as the app's voice rules require). A review that lists failures would fall into the self-focused feedback trap from Kluger & DeNisi.
- Combining the review with the fresh start of Monday is a natural design: Sunday evening or Monday morning, two questions, one choice.
- Transfer to ADHD: uncertain. Reflection relies on memory of the week, which can be harder with ADHD; the app's own log (starts, play minutes) can supply the facts so the review is about meaning, not recall.

### Gaps
- To verify: co-author Francesca Gino was the subject of research misconduct findings at Harvard on other papers (2023 onward); this paper was not among those I know of, but its data provenance was not checked here.
- The exact reflection routine (how long, how often) and whether 22.8% is relative or in points should be confirmed in the paper.
- No study of weekly reviews in ADHD or in personal planners was found.
