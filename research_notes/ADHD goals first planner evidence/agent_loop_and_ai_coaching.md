## 1. Just-in-time adaptive interventions (JITAIs): the framework and how well they work

### Takeaway
A JITAI is the formal name for what the Follower already is: software that checks a person's situation at set moments and picks the right kind of help (or no help) by a written rule. Pooled effects look moderate in early meta-analyses (g about 0.9 versus non-JITAI treatments, Wang and Miller 2020) but much smaller in the most recent, stricter mental health meta-analysis (g = 0.15, von Lützow et al. 2025), and the physical activity review found no adequately powered trial. Evidence strength: moderate that JITAIs can help, low on how much; almost none in ADHD.

### Cited Findings
**The framework (Nahum-Shani et al.)**
- Citation: Nahum-Shani I, Smith SN, Spring BJ, Collins LM, Witkiewitz K, Tewari A, Murphy SA. "Just-in-Time Adaptive Interventions (JITAIs) in Mobile Health: Key Components and Design Principles for Ongoing Health Behavior Support." Annals of Behavioral Medicine 2018;52(6):446-462 (online first 2016/2017). [PMC record](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC5364076)
- The paper names six parts of a JITAI: a distal outcome, proximal outcomes, decision points, intervention options, tailoring variables and decision rules. [PMC record](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC5364076)
- Definitions as summarized from the paper: decision points are the moments when an intervention decision has to be made; tailoring variables are information about the person used to decide when and/or how to intervene; intervention options are the set of possible treatments or actions available at a decision point; decision rules link the tailoring variables to the options in a systematic way. [PMC record](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC5364076)
- "State of receptivity" is described (via a secondary review citing Nahum-Shani et al.) as the person's ability to receive, process and use the support provided. The exact wording in the 2018 paper could not be checked in this session. [Secondary review, PMC9494762](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC9494762); [Michigan ISR project on states of vulnerability and receptivity](https://src.isr.umich.edu/research/projects/novel-use-of-mhealth-data-to-identify-states-of-vulnerability-and-receptivity-to-jitais/)
- A related 2015 Nahum-Shani paper on building health behavior models has a section on the state of receptivity grounded in the dual-process theory of supportive communication. [PMC4732268](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4732268)

**Effectiveness: meta-analyses and reviews**
- Wang L, Miller LC. "Just-in-the-Moment Adaptive Interventions (JITAI): A Meta-Analytical Review." Health Communication (DOI 10.1080/10410236.2019.1652388; online 2019, issue 2020). 33 empirical studies. JITAI versus waitlist control: Hedges' g = 1.65 (k = 9). JITAI versus non-JITAI treatments: g = 0.89 (k = 21). Within-group change in JITAI arms: g = 0.79 (k = 13). Effects held in sensitivity tests and across behaviors (for example blood glucose control, recovering alcoholics), treatment length and age. [Publisher abstract](https://informahealthcare.com/doi/abs/10.1080/10410236.2019.1652388); authorship confirmed by a later scoping review [PMC11583291](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC11583291/)
- A 2023 meta-analysis in Digital Health ("Using a complexity science approach to evaluate the effectiveness of just-in-time adaptive interventions") found a medium effect on objectively measured behavior change, g = 0.77 (95% CI 0.32 to 1.22), from 21 effect sizes; design features (theory base, target behavior, passive versus active sensing) did not moderate the effect. [UvA repository record](https://dare.uva.nl/id/4c8814cc-b448-46e0-87b1-30ef52385731); [NAU record](https://experts.nau.edu/en/publications/using-a-complexity-science-approach-to-evaluate-the-effectiveness/)
- von Lützow U, Neuendorf NL, Scherr S. Systematic review and meta-analysis of JITAIs and ecological momentary interventions for mental health, BMJ Mental Health 2025;28:e301641. K = 23 studies, N = 2,563 (71.7% female), published 2018 to May 2025. Pooled between-group effect g = 0.15 (95% CI 0.05 to 0.26, p = 0.003). Nine studies with follow-up (mean 3.06 months) still showed effects at 1 and 3 to 6 months. Interventions shorter than 6 weeks showed longer-lasting effects (g = 0.71, 95% CI 0.18 to 1.24; subgroup). Risk of bias moderate to high for adherence and missing data. The authors call for a clear definition of JITAIs and their decision rules and for careful choice of control conditions. [Bamberg repository](https://fis.uni-bamberg.de/handle/uniba/116116)
- Hardeman W, Houghton J, Lane K, Jones A, Naughton F. "A systematic review of just-in-time adaptive interventions (JITAIs) to promote physical activity." International Journal of Behavioral Nutrition and Physical Activity 2019;16:31. 19 papers, 14 unique JITAIs, 6 randomized studies. Evidence on behavior change was mixed and no study was powered to detect effects. Users generally found them acceptable; problems were technology, sensor reliability and the timeliness of "just-in-time" messages. Common techniques: goal setting, prompts or cues, feedback, action planning; only 5 studies reported a theory base. The authors advise caution in recommending JITAIs until better evidence exists, while judging harm unlikely. [IJBNPA PDF](https://ijbnpa.biomedcentral.com/counter/pdf/10.1186/s12966-019-0792-7.pdf); [PMC6448257](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC6448257/)

### Inferences
- Plain version for Marcelo: a JITAI is "if this is happening, then do that, otherwise do nothing", checked at fixed moments. The Follower maps onto it directly:
  - Decision points: every 15-second check, each game end, the 25 and 45 minute marks, about 10 minutes of planning without a start.
  - Tailoring variables: meeting now or within 45 minutes, a planned task overdue and open, tasks due with no plan, play minutes today, calm-return day.
  - Intervention options: tight mode (strong chess, 6 by 6 Sudoku), free mode, the after-game "Start 1 minute", the 25/45 minute nudge, the planning line, and doing nothing.
  - Decision rules: `playInput()`, `playMode`, `chessLevel` and the other rules in `rules.js`.
  - Proximal outcome: a start within 10 minutes. Distal outcome: start delay and weekly goal progress.
- The drop from g = 0.89 (2020, mixed comparisons) to g = 0.15 (2025, mental health, stricter controls) is the usual pattern as evidence matures. A realistic expectation for any single nudge is "small, and possibly zero", which is why item 2 (testing it on himself) matters.
- "Doing nothing" is a real option in the framework, and the "never while he types, during a break, with a window open" guards in `followTick` are receptivity rules in this sense.

### Gaps
- No JITAI trial in adults with ADHD was found. All pooled effects above come from non-ADHD populations (physical activity, substance use, diabetes, general mental health).
- The exact wording of the 2018 definitions for "state of vulnerability/opportunity" and "state of receptivity" could not be read in the full paper (host blocked).
- The publication year of Wang and Miller is listed as 2019 online; the final issue year (2020) and page numbers were not verified.

## 2. Micro-randomized trials, and how one person can test whether a nudge works

### Takeaway
A micro-randomized trial (MRT) flips a weighted coin at every decision point to decide whether to send a nudge, then compares what happened right after "sent" versus "not sent". HeartSteps showed that nudges can work at first and then fade: walking suggestions raised steps in the next 30 minutes by 107% at the start of the study, 24% on average, and the effect shrank over 6 weeks. The same logic works for one person (an N-of-1 or single-case randomized design), because the unit being randomized is the moment, not the person. Evidence strength: strong for the method; moderate for nudge effects in general; no ADHD data.

### Cited Findings
**The method**
- Klasnja P, Hekler EB, Shiffman S, Boruvka A, Almirall D, Tewari A, Murphy SA. "Microrandomized trials: An experimental design for developing just-in-time adaptive interventions." Health Psychology 2015;34(Suppl):1220-1228. DOI 10.1037/hea0000305. The design allows causal modeling of the immediate (proximal) effects of randomized components and of how those effects change over time. [Author PDF](https://archive.md2k.org/images/papers/jitai/nihms732638_Klasnja.pdf)
- Klasnja cautioned that MRTs only test "push" interventions (things the app sends), not content the user opens at will. [NIH OBSSR summary](https://obssr.od.nih.gov/news-and-events/news/microrandomized-trials-just-time-mhealth-designs-get-granular)
- UK government evaluation guidance describes MRTs as repeatedly randomizing within each person to tailor components such as when to send a prompt, with HeartSteps as the worked example. [gov.uk guidance](https://gov.uk/guidance/micro-randomised-trial)
- Methods papers on MRT design and analysis: Qian T, Walton AE, Collins LM, et al. "The Micro-Randomized Trial for Developing Digital Interventions: Experimental Design and Data Analysis Considerations" [arXiv 2107.03544](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2107.03544); and "Data Analysis Methods" [arXiv 2004.10241](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2004.10241). One methods source notes that if people habituate to suggestions or find them burdensome, the causal effect is expected to deteriorate over time. [arXiv 2107.03544](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2107.03544)

**HeartSteps results**
- Klasnja P, Smith S, Seewald NJ, Lee A, Hall K, Luers B, Hekler EB, Murphy SA. "Efficacy of Contextually Tailored Suggestions for Physical Activity: A Micro-randomized Optimization Trial of HeartSteps." Annals of Behavioral Medicine 2019;53(6):573-582. DOI 10.1093/abm/kay067. 44 adults, 6 weeks, 5 decision points per day; at each one the app randomized whether to send a suggestion and, if so, a walking or an anti-sedentary one. [PubMed abstract](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/30192907/)
- Averaged over days and types, a suggestion versus none raised the 30-minute step count by 14% (35 steps over a 253-step average), p = .06 (borderline). [PubMed abstract](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/30192907/)
- Any suggestion initially raised steps by 66% (167 steps, p < .01), and this effect diminished over time. [PubMed abstract](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/30192907/)
- Walking suggestions: average +24% (59 steps, p = .02); largest at the start, +107% (271 steps), then decreasing. Anti-sedentary suggestions: no detectable effect. [PubMed abstract](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/30192907/)
- The trial registry listed as a secondary hypothesis that the proximal effect would decrease with time in the study. [Trial record](https://ctv.veeva.com/study/a-micro-randomized-trial-of-heartsteps-an-mhealth-intervention-for-increasing-physical-activity); a 2024 reanalysis treats the shape of the decay (linear or not) as still open. [arXiv 2410.15049](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2410.15049)

**Another MRT on notifications and engagement**
- Bidargaddi N, et al. "To Prompt or Not to Prompt? A Microrandomized Trial of Time-Varying Push Notifications to Increase Proximal Engagement With a Mobile Health App." JMIR mHealth and uHealth 2018;6(11):e10123. 1,255 users of a workplace well-being app, 89 days. A tailored push message raised the chance of app use in the next 24 hours by about 3.9% (risk ratio 1.039). Largest at 12:30 PM on weekends (+11.8%, 90% CI 1.02 to 1.13). Weekend +8.7% versus weekday +2.5% (difference not significant, P = .18). The effect shrank over the study, not significantly. [JMIR](https://mhealth.jmir.org/2018/11/e10123)

**One person: N-of-1 and single-case randomized designs**
- An N-of-1 trial is a randomized single-person crossover: one person goes through several conditions, alternating, with outcomes measured in each period. [arXiv 2208.00739](https://pdf.arxiv.org/pdf/2208.00739); [Brain Boost protocol, PMC6996754](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC6996754/)
- A 2022 paper on within-individual causal inference states that its framework also covers counterfactual formalization of micro-randomized trials, that is, treating an MRT as a within-person causal design. [arXiv 2208.00739](https://pdf.arxiv.org/pdf/2208.00739)
- In a randomized alternating treatments design, conditions switch rapidly and the order is randomized. Because the randomized unit is the measurement occasion, a single person can support a valid randomization test. Block or restricted randomization are options. [Behavior Research Methods, s13428-018-1084-x](https://link.springer.com/content/pdf/10.3758/s13428-018-1084-x.pdf); [Tanious and Onghena on randomized single-case designs in healthcare, PMC6768928](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6768928)
- A simulation study found the randomization test (with a suitable statistic) more powerful than visual-only comparisons, while a simple binomial test was too liberal; SCRIBE (Tate et al. 2016) is the reporting guideline for single-case studies. [Behavior Research Methods](https://link.springer.com/content/pdf/10.3758/s13428-018-1084-x.pdf)
- An app-based N-of-1 study (Brain Boost) randomized notification frequency (light versus moderate) as a factor, focused on completion and adherence. [Frontiers in Computer Science](https://www.frontiersin.org/articles/10.3389/fcomp.2020.00004/pdf); [PMC6996754](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC6996754/)

### Inferences
- Plain version: to know whether the after-game "Start 1 minute" nudge helps, the app can flip a coin each time a game ends. Heads: show the nudge as today. Tails: show the plain after-game screen with no nudge line (the start button can stay in its usual place so nothing important is hidden). Log which one he got and whether a start followed within 10 minutes. After enough games, compare the two start rates. Because the coin decided, a difference is caused by the nudge, not by the kind of day he was having.
- Simplest valid version for one person (my design suggestion, not from a source):
  1. Pick one nudge and one outcome (for example the 25-minute play nudge, or the after-game line; outcome: start within 10 minutes).
  2. Randomize at each eligible moment with a fixed probability (0.5 is simplest; 0.6 "show" if withholding feels costly).
  3. Log: time, show or withhold, the tailoring state (tight or free, meeting soon), and the outcome. The app already logs `nudge` and `play` moments; only the coin result needs adding.
  4. Decide the comparison before starting and do not peek and stop early.
  5. Count only moments when he was "available" (app open, not typing, no window open), as MRTs do.
- Rough sample size (my calculation with a standard two-proportion formula, 5% significance, 80% power, ignoring day-to-day correlation, so treat as a floor): to detect a rise from 30% to 50% starting within 10 minutes needs about 93 moments per arm (about 186 in total); from 20% to 40% needs about 81 per arm. At 3 to 5 eligible game ends per day this is roughly 6 to 9 weeks. A larger true effect would show sooner. The 25 and 45 minute nudges fire at most twice a day, so they would take many months to test alone; the after-game line is the practical one to test.
- A simpler but weaker alternative: randomize by day (nudges on or off for the whole day, the alternating treatments design). Easier to understand, but it gives one data point per day, so it needs far more calendar time.
- HeartSteps and Bidargaddi both show effects that fade. If a nudge "works" in week 1, keep the coin running to see whether it still works in week 6. Changing the wording now and then is a reasonable response, but each change restarts the test.
- Withholding is ethically easy here because the nudges are low-stakes, the app never blocks, and he consents to his own test.

### Gaps
- No published single-person MRT of a productivity or ADHD nudge was found.
- The exact decline rate in HeartSteps (per day) was not in the abstract; the full paper was blocked.
- Guidance specific to self-run experiments (how to handle day-level correlation for one person) was not found; a statistician would refine the sample size.

## 3. Receptivity, interruption cost and how much prompting is too much

### Takeaway
Whether a nudge lands depends strongly on the moment: time of day, whether the person is already using the phone, activity and location all predict response, and machine-learned timing improved receptivity by up to 40% over random timing. Interruptions make people work faster but with more stress, and a message seen as disruptive is more likely to be dismissed. No study gives a universal "maximum number of prompts"; the evidence supports few, well-timed prompts and watching for decay. Evidence strength: moderate; all from non-ADHD samples.

### Cited Findings
- Künzler F, Mishra V, Kramer JN, Kotz D, Fleisch E, Kowatsch T. "Exploring the State-of-Receptivity for mHealth Interventions." Proc. ACM IMWUT 2019;3(4), Article 140, DOI 10.1145/3369805. 189 participants, 6 weeks, physical activity interventions delivered by the chatbot coach "Ally". Day and time, phone battery, phone interaction, physical activity and location were significantly associated with in-the-moment receptivity; the authors also suggest that being receptive helped participants reach their activity goals. [Dartmouth project page](https://cs.dartmouth.edu/dfk/research/kunzler-receptivity/index.html); [PMC9494762](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC9494762)
- Ally was a text chatbot on Android and iOS that also gathered context from phone sensors; its MRT tested self-monitoring prompts, planning and incentives. [JMIR Research Protocols protocol, PMC6374735](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC6374735/)
- Mishra V, Künzler F, Kramer JN, Fleisch E, Kowatsch T, Kotz D. "Detecting Receptivity for mHealth Interventions in the Natural Environment." Proc. ACM IMWUT, June 2021. 83 participants, 3 weeks. Machine-learning timing models gave up to a 40% improvement in receptivity compared with a control that sent messages at random times; an adaptive model that kept learning became more effective over the study. (A separate "77%" figure refers to classifier F1 score, not receptivity.) [arXiv 2011.08302](https://arxiv.org/abs/2011.08302)
- Mehrotra A, Pejovic V, Vermeulen J, Hendley R, Musolesi M. "My Phone and Me: Understanding People's Receptivity to Mobile Notifications." CHI 2016, pp. 1021-1032. Experience sampling app; 10,372 notifications and 474 questionnaire responses. Factors studied: alert type, sender, presentation, ongoing task, perceived disruption, response time. The higher the perceived disruption, the more likely the notification is dismissed; the authors conclude receptivity needs highly personalized handling. [UCL PDF](https://discovery.ucl.ac.uk/1502364/1/CHI16.pdf)
- Mark G, Gudith D, Klocke U. "The Cost of Interrupted Work: More Speed and Stress." CHI 2008, pp. 107-110. Interrupted tasks were finished faster at similar quality, but people reported more stress, frustration, effort and time pressure; the authors read this as compensating for interruptions at a cost. [Paper PDF](https://www.interruptions.net/literature/Mark-CHI08.pdf)
- Bidargaddi et al. 2018 (above): the best moment for an engagement prompt in a well-being app was midday on weekends, and the average effect of a prompt was small (+3.9%). [JMIR](https://mhealth.jmir.org/2018/11/e10123)
- Hardeman et al. 2019 (above) list the timeliness of just-in-time messages among the main feasibility problems. [IJBNPA PDF](https://ijbnpa.biomedcentral.com/counter/pdf/10.1186/s12966-019-0792-7.pdf)

### Inferences
- The Follower already follows the main receptivity findings: it speaks at natural breaks (game end), never while he types or has a window open, and caps the play nudges at two per day. These are the right instincts.
- The 15-second check itself costs him nothing because it is silent; only moments when it speaks count as burden. It would be worth logging "speaks per day" and "dismissed without action" as a burden measure. A rising dismiss rate is the sign of too much.
- For the weekly AI coach, the receptivity lesson is to pick a fixed, predictable moment he chooses (for example Sunday at a set hour) rather than interrupting.
- ADHD may change these patterns (more distraction from interruptions, but also more need for external cues); no study found tests this directly.

### Gaps
- No study found sets a numeric ceiling on prompts per day.
- No receptivity or interruption study in adults with ADHD was found.
- The detailed effect sizes in Künzler 2019 (for example how much response rates differed by time of day) could not be read; host blocked.

## 4. Digital interventions for adult ADHD and for procrastination

### Takeaway
For adult ADHD, a handful of small RCTs of internet-delivered CBT-style programs show moderate self-reported symptom gains (for example d = 0.70 in Kenter et al. 2023, N = 120), but pooled effects across digital interventions are small (g = -0.25 on inattention across 23 RCTs), adherence is weak (29% finished all modules), and most commercial ADHD apps have no efficacy evidence at all. For procrastination, internet CBT showed moderate effects in one RCT (N = 150, d about 0.70) and a meta-analysis found small overall benefit (g = 0.34) with CBT doing better (g = 0.55, few studies). Evidence strength: low to moderate; samples small, outcomes self-reported, and the procrastination trials were mostly not in people with ADHD.

### Cited Findings
**Adult ADHD: trials**
- Kenter RMF, Gjestad R, Lundervold AJ, Nordgreen T. "A self-guided internet-delivered intervention for adults with ADHD: Results from a randomized controlled trial." Internet Interventions 2023;32:100614. N = 120 adults with self-reported ADHD diagnosis; self-guided program (7 modules) versus online psychoeducation. Between-group effects: ADHD symptoms (Adult ADHD Self-Report Scale) d = 0.70, quality of life d = 0.53; at 3-month follow-up d = 0.76 and d = 0.52. Adherence: 29% completed all modules, 59% completed at least 5 of 7. Registered NCT04726813. [DOAJ](https://doaj.org/article/2aa213698a7543d9be32d2b1823bb6ad); [ClinicalTrials.gov](https://clinicaltrials.gov/study/NCT04726813)
- Nordby ES, et al. 2021 (Internet Interventions): uncontrolled feasibility study, 13 participants with ADHD given the first 3 of 7 modules of the same self-guided program; 7 (54%) completed all 3; good credibility and satisfaction and a significant drop in inattention, which the authors say must be read with caution. [Internet Interventions via DOAJ](https://doaj.org/article/fa4da7506d704d2596ac292b2918b109); [Bergen repository](https://bora.uib.no/bora-xmlui/handle/11250/2768789?show=full)
- Pettersson R, et al. 2017 (Sweden): RCT with 45 adults with ADHD randomized to self-guided internet CBT, internet CBT with weekly group meetings, or waitlist. A secondary review reports the group-meeting arm's difference was not statistically significant. A Cochrane table flags high risk of conflict of interest (the principal author was a partner and shareholder in the company that owns the program). [Trial registry NCT02041884](https://clinicaltrials.gov/study/NCT02041884); [Cochrane table](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6494390/table/CD010840-tblf-0008)

**Adult ADHD: reviews**
- A meta-analysis of 23 RCTs of digital mental health interventions for ADHD (99 effect sizes, 1,472 participants; author list points to 2025) found a small pooled effect on inattention, g = -0.25 (95% CI -0.47 to -0.04), no significant difference between therapist-guided and self-guided programs, lower effects for neurofeedback than for combination interventions, and no clear benefit from telehealth and mHealth tools. Whether it is limited to adults was not visible. [Oxford repository](https://ora.ox.ac.uk/objects/uuid:fe5c55cb-4e47-4676-9ab3-45c89ab049f7/files/rjh343v81p)
- Earlier meta-analysis of online interventions for ADHD (2022): 6 RCTs, 261 patients including adults, suggested benefit. [PMC9274127](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC9274127/)
- Păsărelu CR, Andersson G, Dobrean A. "Attention-deficit/hyperactivity disorder mobile apps: A systematic review." International Journal of Medical Informatics 2020. Of 355 apps found in the App Store, Google Play and the NHS library, 109 were reviewed; very few described how they were developed and none reported evidence of efficacy or effectiveness. [PubMed record](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/32283479)
- A 2025 systematic review of ADHD management apps notes adult RCTs in which a psychoeducational app beat brochure-based psychoeducation on inattention and impulsivity, and a chatbot-delivered psychoeducation app beat an information-only control for adults with attention problems. [PMC11904913](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC11904913/)
- A review of 26 systematic reviews (BMC Psychiatry, 2025) concluded digital ADHD interventions show potential, but effectiveness remains inconclusive because evidence quality is low. [DOAJ](https://doaj.org/article/789ef3c13fd143468097858bb49f61e5)
- A 2024 UK report on adult ADHD digital tools combined 13 peer-reviewed interventions (14 studies) with 10 commercial apps; apps were scientifically grounded (CBT, psychoeducation) but most lacked RCT support in adults. [Strathclyde repository](https://strathprints.strath.ac.uk/89559/)
- A 2025 systematic review of digital CBT apps for ADHD (children and adults) reported promising symptom and adherence results; not a formal meta-analysis. [PMC12436941](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC12436941/)

**Procrastination**
- Rozental A, Forsell E, Svensson A, Andersson G, Carlbring P. "Internet-based cognitive-behavior therapy for procrastination: A randomized controlled trial." Journal of Consulting and Clinical Psychology 2015;83(4):808-824, DOI 10.1037/ccp0000023. N = 150 randomized to guided self-help, unguided self-help, or waitlist. Both active arms showed moderate between-group effects versus waitlist; one reported Pure Procrastination Scale effect is d = 0.70 (95% CI 0.29 to 1.10), and Irrational Procrastination Scale effects of d = 0.81 and d = 0.69 appear; which arm each belongs to could not be confirmed from the abstract. The authors conclude internet CBT can help with self-reported procrastination with or without a therapist. [PubMed record](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/25939016/)
- Rozental A, Bennett S, Forsström D, et al. "Targeting Procrastination Using Psychological Treatments: A Systematic Review and Meta-Analysis." Frontiers in Psychology 2018;9:1588. 12 studies (21 comparisons, N = 718), only RCTs versus inactive controls with self-report outcomes. Overall g = 0.34 (95% CI 0.11 to 0.56), I squared = 61%. CBT subgroup g = 0.55 (95% CI 0.32 to 0.77), I squared = 0%, but based on very few studies. Egger's test not significant. [Frontiers](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/psychology/articles/10.3389/fpsyg.2018.01588/full)

### Inferences
- Plain version: structured online programs that teach ADHD and procrastination skills help some adults a moderate amount, by their own report, and many people stop partway. Generic apps sold for ADHD mostly have no proof. A planner like his is in the "no proof yet" group, which is why logging and self-testing are worth more than adding features.
- Low completion (29% finished Kenter's program) fits the project's design choices: short screens, one action, easy return after time away.
- The CBT parts that recur across these programs (planning, breaking tasks into first steps, managing distraction, self-compassion) are already in the app as the first small step, "Start 1 minute" and the calm-return rules.

### Gaps
- The Rozental 2015 trial was in a general self-selected sample, not people with ADHD. No procrastination-specific digital trial in adults with ADHD was found.
- The Journal of Affective Disorders 2024 meta-analysis of digital ADHD interventions (Liu et al.) was located only as a catalog record; findings and age range were not visible.
- Effect sizes for Pettersson 2017 were not retrieved.

## 5. AI and chatbot coaching: what controlled studies show

### Takeaway
Rule-based and AI chatbots reduce depression and distress in pooled RCTs (Li et al. 2023: g = 0.64 and 0.70; but He et al. 2023: g = 0.29, short-term only), and the first RCT of a generative AI therapy bot (Therabot, NEJM AI 2025, N = 210) found significant symptom drops versus waitlist. For goals specifically, a rule-based coaching bot raised goal attainment versus control (Terblanche et al. 2022), and an LLM coach (Bloom, 2025) raised app use about fivefold but did not beat the same app without the LLM on actual activity. There is no outcome trial of AI coaching for adults with ADHD. Evidence strength: moderate for mental health symptoms (non-ADHD), low for goal pursuit, none for ADHD.

### Cited Findings
**Meta-analyses**
- Li H, Zhang R, Lee YC, Kraut RE, Mohr DC. Depression g = 0.64 (95% CI 0.17 to 1.12); distress g = 0.70 (95% CI 0.18 to 1.22); well-being not significant, g = 0.32 (95% CI -0.13 to 0.78). More time in the app is not the goal here (the app is meant to send him toward work).
- Bloom's result is the most relevant warning for his plan: the parts that moved behavior in both arms were the non-AI pieces (goal setting, action planning, tracking, reminders), which his app already has. The AI's added value may be in feelings (self-compassion, enjoyment) and in drafting, not in the doing.

- Heinz's "not ready to operate fully autonomously" supports a design where the AI proposes and he decides.

## 6. Risks of AI help with goals: over-reliance, ownership, sycophancy, email privacy

### Takeaway
Goals pursued for one's own reasons are pursued harder and pay off more in well-being (self-concordance), and people value what they help make (IKEA effect), so goals suggested wholesale by an AI risk being weaker goals. Language models also tend to agree with the user (sycophancy), which undercuts accountability, and early studies link heavy AI use with less sense of ownership and more offloading (correlational or small). Reading email with an AI that can also act outward is a known security pattern for data leaks. Evidence strength: strong for self-concordance; moderate for IKEA and sycophancy; weak and preliminary for AI over-reliance; the email risk is an engineering consensus, not a trial result.

### Cited Findings
- Sheldon KM, Elliot AJ. "Goal Striving, Need Satisfaction, and Longitudinal Well-Being: The Self-Concordance Model." Journal of Personality and Social Psychology 1999;76(3):482-497. Goals consistent with a person's interests and core values get more sustained effort and so are more often attained, and attaining them brings larger well-being gains, mediated by daily autonomy, competence and relatedness. Goals pursued for controlled reasons bring little well-being benefit even when attained. [PubMed record](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/10101878/)
- A 2024 systematic review and meta-analysis (Sezer et al., Motivation Science) found self-concordant goals associated with more goal effort and attainment, need satisfaction and well-being. [Southampton repository](https://eprints.soton.ac.uk/493985)
- Norton MI, Mochon D, Ariely D. "The IKEA effect: When labor leads to love." Journal of Consumer Psychology 2012;22(3):453-460. People who assembled IKEA boxes, origami or Lego valued their own amateur creations close to experts' versions; the effect disappeared when they failed to finish or destroyed what they built. [SSRN](https://papers.ssrn.com/abstract=1777100); [Harvard DASH](https://dash.harvard.edu/handle/1/12136084)
- Sharma M, Tong M, Korbak T, et al. "Towards Understanding Sycophancy in Language Models." ICLR 2024 (arXiv Oct 2023, Anthropic authors). Five AI assistants consistently showed sycophancy across four tasks; matching the user's views was one of the strongest predictors of which answer humans preferred; humans and preference models sometimes preferred convincing sycophantic answers over correct ones. The authors conclude sycophancy is a general property of models trained on human feedback, driven partly by human preference. [arXiv](https://arxiv.org/abs/2310.13548v4); [ICLR proceedings](https://proceedings.iclr.cc/paper_files/paper/2024/hash/0105f7972202c1d4fb817da9f21a9663-Abstract-Conference.html)
- Gerlich M. 2025 (Societies): survey of 666 people; more AI use went with more cognitive offloading and lower critical-thinking scores, strongest in younger people. Correlational; the reverse direction is possible. [PsyPost summary](https://psypost.org/2025/03/ai-tools-may-weaken-critical-thinking-skills-by-encouraging-cognitive-offloading-study-suggests-227092)
- MIT Media Lab preprint "Your Brain on ChatGPT" (2025): 54 students over four months writing essays; the ChatGPT group showed weaker neural connectivity (EEG), weaker memory of their text and a lower sense of ownership of their writing, and relied on it more uncritically over sessions ("cognitive debt"). Small, not peer reviewed at the time of reporting; source is press coverage. [Laptop Mag coverage](https://www.laptopmag.com/ai/chatgpt-mit-study-brain-activity-memory-impact)
- Bloom (2025, above): the LLM arm used the app about five times as long without more activity, which is engagement without outcome. [arXiv](https://arxiv.org/abs/2510.05449v1)
- Willison S. "The lethal trifecta for AI agents" (June 16, 2025): an AI system that has (1) access to private data, (2) exposure to untrusted content and (3) a way to communicate externally can be tricked into leaking data. Email is the standard case: anyone can email instructions to the AI, and it can send data back out. His advice for users is to avoid that combination. [Simon Willison's weblog](https://simonwillison.net/2025/Jun/16/the-lethal-trifecta/)
- A security vendor report treats inbox-reading assistants as the textbook case, cites Microsoft's "EchoLeak" (disclosed June 2025) as the first widely publicized zero-click AI data-leak flaw, and says no known technique reliably prevents prompt injection in a general-purpose agent, so defense has to come from architecture. Vendor source. [Nightfall](https://nightfall.ai/blog/how-do-ai-agents-create-data-exfiltration-risk)

### Inferences
- Plain version: if the AI writes his goals, they may feel like someone else's homework. Better: the AI asks questions and offers two or three options; he writes or edits the final words and the "why" (the app already requires a "why" for each goal). That keeps the goal his (self-concordance) and the effort his (IKEA effect).
- Weekly goals drawn from email and calendar are a special risk: email is mostly other people's requests, so goals built from it may pull toward controlled ("I have to") reasons. The weekly prompt can ask him to mark which suggestions he wants versus which are obligations.
- Sycophancy undercuts accountability. The pasted prompt should tell his AI to point out when the week's plan does not fit his hours, to name one thing to drop, and not to praise. Even then, expect agreement bias.
- His paste-based email setup already breaks the "lethal trifecta" on the planner's side: his own AI reads the mail and writes a small `.md` file, and the planner turns it into cards with plain rules, holding no email access and sending nothing out. The leak risk then lives in his own AI tool (if it reads mail and can also browse or send). Keeping the `.md` format narrow (task names and dates only) limits what an injected instruction in an email could carry into the app.
- Over-reliance concern for ADHD: an always-available helper can become one more place to procrastinate (planning about planning). The existing over-planning nudge is a guard against this.

### Gaps
- No study found tests AI-suggested versus self-chosen goals directly (the ownership risk is an extrapolation from self-concordance and IKEA research).
- No study of sycophancy in coaching or accountability conversations specifically was found.
- The MIT study could only be read through press coverage.

## 7. What an "AI agent in a loop" is, and the three ways to build one

### Takeaway
An agent loop is a cycle: observe, decide, act, log, repeat. Anthropic's engineering guidance separates "workflows" (fixed steps written in code, with an AI call at chosen points) from "agents" (the AI decides its own steps and tool use in a loop), and advises the simplest solution that works, because agents trade cost and latency for capability and can compound errors. For his app, the evidence and the guidance both point to: keep the 15-second rules loop (free), add a scheduled weekly AI check as a workflow, and avoid an always-on AI agent. Evidence strength: this is engineering guidance, not trial evidence.

### Cited Findings
- Anthropic, "Building effective agents" (Erik Schluntz and Barry Zhang, Dec 19, 2024). Workflows are "systems where LLMs and tools are orchestrated through predefined code paths"; agents are "systems where LLMs dynamically direct their own processes and tool usage." [Anthropic](https://www.anthropic.com/engineering/building-effective-agents)
- Same source: find "the simplest solution possible, and only increasing complexity when needed"; agentic systems "often trade latency and cost for better task performance." [Anthropic](https://www.anthropic.com/engineering/building-effective-agents)
- Same source: agents are "typically just LLMs using tools based on environmental feedback in a loop," need "ground truth" from the environment at each step, can "pause for human feedback at checkpoints or when encountering blockers," and commonly include "stopping conditions (such as a maximum number of iterations)." [Anthropic](https://www.anthropic.com/engineering/building-effective-agents)
- Same source: autonomy brings "higher costs, and the potential for compounding errors"; the authors recommend "extensive testing in sandboxed environments, along with the appropriate guardrails." [Anthropic](https://www.anthropic.com/engineering/building-effective-agents)
- The JITAI literature's call for clear, written decision rules (von Lützow et al. 2025) and the MRT requirement that each decision be logged with its context (Klasnja et al. 2015) describe the same observe, decide, act, log cycle from the health research side. [Bamberg repository](https://fis.uni-bamberg.de/handle/uniba/116116); [Klasnja 2015 PDF](https://archive.md2k.org/images/papers/jitai/nihms732638_Klasnja.pdf)

### Inferences
- Plain version of the loop, with his app as the example:
  1. Observe: read the day (calendar, open tasks, minutes played, minutes planning).
  2. Decide: apply a rule ("a meeting starts within 45 minutes, so tight mode").
  3. Act: change the game, show a line, or do nothing.
  4. Log: save what was decided and what he did next (`nudge`, `play`, `start` moments).
  5. Repeat.
- Three ways to run it:
  - **Rules loop (what the Follower is now).** Decisions are written rules in `rules.js`. Cost: zero, runs offline, instant, predictable, testable with unit tests. Failure modes: it only knows what its rules cover; rules can be wrong in ways nobody notices without the logs; too many rules become hard to reason about.
  - **Scheduled AI check (a workflow).** Once a day or once a week, a fixed sequence runs: gather a summary (the Follower summary, calendar, the email `.md`), send one prompt to an AI, get a draft back, and he approves or edits. Cost: one AI call per run (about 4 to 52 per month for daily or weekly; with his paste approach, the cost is his own time and his existing AI subscription). Failure modes: a bad or sycophantic draft (mitigated by him approving), stale input if he skips a week, privacy of what goes in the summary, and drift if the prompt is changed often.
  - **Always-on AI agent.** An AI sits in the loop and decides each step itself. At the Follower's 15-second pace for a 10-hour day that would be about 2,400 AI calls a day (my arithmetic), each with delay, cost and some chance of a wrong or odd action. Failure modes named by Anthropic: higher cost, compounding errors, the need for stopping conditions and guardrails. It also needs an API key and a server or always-open tab, which his app does not have and his rules forbid adding without approval.
- Recommended shape, consistent with all the above: rules decide "when" (cheap, testable, every 15 seconds); a weekly AI workflow helps with "what" (suggesting weekly goals from his life goals, calendar and email `.md`); he is the checkpoint that approves; the logs (and the coin-flip test in item 2) say whether any of it works. This matches "start simple, add complexity only when it shows clear benefit".
- An accountability check-in can itself be rules-based (Vici in item 5 was rule-based and improved goal attainment): a weekly screen that shows last week's chosen goals, what was started, and asks for one next action. The AI only drafts the suggestions.

### Gaps
- No study was found comparing rules-based, scheduled-AI and always-on-AI versions of a coaching system head to head.
- Specific current AI pricing was not researched (not needed while he uses no API key); if a key is added later, current per-token pricing should be checked at that time.
